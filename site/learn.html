A guided course · @COUNT@ papers · one relation

The Emission Algebra,
and everything that grows from it.

Every paper in this archive is a branch of one small fact about a single matrix. This course walks the tree from its root.

You do not need to read the @COUNT@ papers in the order they were written. They grow from one relation — a matrix R with R² = R + I — and the fastest way to understand any one of them is to see where it sits on that tree. This page is the map and the path: seven modules, each teaching the ideas in prose, then handing you the papers that prove them and the code that checks them. Nothing here is asserted that a machine has not verified in exact arithmetic.

R² = R + I

The same relation the golden ratio obeys: φ² = φ + 1. Read as a matrix it is an algebra; read as a number it is φ = (1+√5)/2 and the field ℚ(√5); read through the Mahler measure it is a cost, floored at φ. Those three readings are Modules 1–3. The rest are what happens when you iterate, learn on, and curve the same object.

How to use this page. Read Module 0 (here) and then go in order — each module states what you'll learn, teaches the load-bearing ideas with the real formulae inline, and closes with a Verify it yourself note and the module's papers (pulled live from the archive, so this list is always current). If you would rather read one continuous narrative, jump to Module 7: the Exact Arithmetic booklet threads the whole corpus into thirteen lessons and is the recommended deep track.

The epistemic tags. Throughout the papers, every claim carries a tag: [forced] (a theorem or an exact computation), [computed] (a validated numerical result), [declared] (a principled, invariance-free modelling choice), or [open]. The house rule that governs all of it: a summary may be shorter than its source, but never stronger.

Module 1

The Two Walls — Mahler, Salem & Lehmer

Mahler, Salem & Lehmer 7 papers

What you'll learn

  • The Mahler measure M(α) = ∏|ζ|>1|ζ| — a multiplicative "cost" on algebraic numbers — and Lehmer's problem: is there a smallest measure above 1?
  • What a Salem number is, and why the numbers this algebra can emit systematically avoid them.
  • The two walls — a height wall and an angle wall — that confine emission to {1} ∪ [φ, ∞) with on-circle eigenvalues only at angles in (π/2)ℤ.
  • Why φ is not assumed but derived, as the floor those walls force.

Attach to each algebraic number a single positive cost: the product of the absolute values of its conjugates that lie outside the unit circle. That is the Mahler measure M. It is 1 exactly for roots of unity, and Lehmer asked in 1933 whether it can come arbitrarily close to 1 without being 1 — a question still open in general.

This corpus does not resolve Lehmer's problem; it does something sharper and complete on its own terrain. The numbers the emission algebra produces are confined by two independent walls. The height wall puts every emitted measure in {1} ∪ [φ, ∞) — there is a clean gap just above 1, and its edge is the golden ratio. The angle wall puts every on-circle eigenvalue at a quarter-turn, an angle in (π/2)ℤ. A Salem number — the classical candidate for a small measure — violates both walls at once, so the entire band (1, μS) is simply absent from the spectral image. Lehmer's problem is sidestepped, not solved: the interesting band is empty here for a structural reason.

The rest of the module is the anatomy of that emptiness. It turns out the "Salem slot" is not merely empty — its vacancy is a conserved ℤ/4ℤ charge, and from that charge alone five separate objects fall out (a grading, the content polynomial x⁴−1, the Mahler floor, a normal form, and a conserved current). Push on the slot and a totally-real Perron occupant appears via the trace redirection β ↦ τ₀ = β + β⁻¹, converging to √5 = φ + φ⁻¹. The dichotomy is tested against the pentagon (ℤ₅) case where the elementary argument fails, and closed at the level of an exhaustive quintic census.

Verify it yourself. Every measure, every census count, and every wall in this module is independently re-derived from the papers' own premises by the archive's test suite (roots to 40+ digits, exact symbolic algebra) — Mahler measures from root products, Galois groups from scratch, never a number copied from the paper and asserted against itself.
Module 2

Into an Algebra — The Spectral Semiring

Spectral Semiring 4 papers

What you'll learn

  • How one matrix R and one relation R² = R + I generate a whole graded algebra 𝔄 — a ring, an 𝔰𝔩₂, and a Mahler-graded semiring — in three layers.
  • The operations ⊕, ⊗ and squaring, and why squaring is the Adams operation ψ² of a λ-ring.
  • The charge–measure coupling: minimal measure at charge group ℤ/n is φ for even n, 2 for odd — the odd gap (φ, 2) is exactly the no-Salem closure.
  • How to make charge relational (reference-free), and the rigidity that pins admissible spectra to anchors n ∈ {m, 2m}.

Module 1 gave us objects and walls; this module gives them an algebra. From the single relation R² = R + I, three exact layers appear at once: the ring ℤ[R] = 𝒪 of integers of ℚ(√5); an 𝔰𝔩₂ of signature (2,1) whose root length is √5; and a Mahler-graded semiring carrying a charge χ ∈ ℤ/4ℤ. They are tied together by one trace duality, ½ Tr(Xₙ²) = 5Fₙ² = Lₙ² − 4(−1)ⁿ, linking Fibonacci and Lucas numbers to the algebra's inner product.

On this algebra the two operations behave like a λ-ring: addition ⊕, tensor ⊗, and a squaring that is precisely the Adams operation ψ² — the diagonal of the tensor square. Two characters read it out: the Mahler measure (additive on ⊕, tropical/max on ⊗) and the ℤ/4ℤ angle charge. The charge–measure coupling is the punchline that explains Module 1's gap arithmetically: the cheapest measure you can carry at charge group ℤ/n is φ when n is even and jumps to 2 when n is odd — and that odd gap (φ, 2) is the same band the Salem walls forbid.

Finally the charge is made relational: the pair charge trel(α,β) ∈ ℚ/ℤ is a groupoid cocycle whose only gauge freedom is the choice of reference ray. Fix nothing external, and a modulus-pinning theorem still leaves every Salem number relationally inert and evaluates the parity floor reference-free — the strongest form of the no-Salem statement.

Verify it yourself. The λ-ring laws, the trace duality, the module dimensions, and the coupling floors are checked in exact sympy in the test suite; the emission-algebra primer ships its own harnesses.
Module 3

Number Fields as Learning Geometry — Residual Return

Residual Return 3 papers

What you'll learn

  • How to read a number field ℚ(θ) as an exact learning geometry: a vector space, a companion-matrix algebra, and a Minkowski lattice glued by the trace form G = MᵀM with det G = dK.
  • The residual-return learner: capture as a vanishing G-orthogonal residual r = x − Px, with Mahler-measure height gating growth.
  • The exchange rate identity λ = 2c, read as second-order KL over the trace-form Fisher metric — and how the golden gate forces λ = √5.

Here the algebra starts to learn. A number field ℚ(θ) is simultaneously a vector space, a companion-matrix algebra, and a lattice under the Minkowski embedding; the trace form G = MᵀM is the metric that glues these views, and its determinant is the field discriminant dK. In that metric, capture — having learned an element — is exactly the vanishing of the G-orthogonal residual r = x − Px, and growth of the learned field is gated by Mahler-measure height with a Smyth-nonzero cost floor. The dynamics have an exact streaming form whose return operator 𝓛(X) = RX + XR − X lands in a φ-slack kernel over the Clifford algebra Cl(2,0) ≅ M₂(ℝ).

The capstone is a single conformal identity. The growth rule's exchange rate — how much description cost you pay per unit of learned structure — is the derived identity λ = 2c, read as a second-order Kullback–Leibler expansion over the trace-form Fisher metric. Čencov's theorem leaves one constant c free; the golden gate fixes it, forcing λ = √5. One sign flip, D = 1 + 4C, inverts the whole regime — the same duality that will reappear as the flip between the two walls.

Verify it yourself. The Fisher matrices are rebuilt from log-likelihood Hessians, the discriminants from determinantal divisors, and the λ = 2c identity checked symbolically, all in the test suite.
Module 4

One Gate, One Ladder, One Helix — The Golden Substrate

Golden Substrate 2 papers

What you'll learn

  • How a single gate iteration fC(x) = C/(1+x) builds a discrete helix.
  • The Lucas–Fibonacci rung ladder ρn = n · ln φ and the state-angle chart on which every height is a gate.
  • Why the whole ladder is the orbit of one complex number q = iτ (with q⁴ = the gap, a ℤ/4ℤ quarter-turn), and why the winding rate κ = π/(2 ln φ) is transcendental.

Take the algebra of Modules 1–3 and iterate it. The gate map fC(x) = C/(1+x) — the continued-fraction step behind φ — turns the static objects into a trajectory: a discrete helix Γ(ρ) climbing a rung ladder whose spacing is ρn = n · ln φ, plotted on a state-angle chart where every integer height is itself a gate. The whitepaper builds this on three declared atoms — the chart, the charge selection, and the winding rate — and shows the helix is explicit and closed-form.

The companion paper compresses it to a single generator: the entire discrete ladder is the orbit of one complex number q = iτ, whose fourth power is exactly the Module-1 gap (a ℤ/4ℤ quarter-turn — the same charge, now geometric). The winding rate κ = π/(2 ln φ) is proved transcendental, and the open problem "OP-RATE" closes as a full classification, with a machine-certified ledger that locates every remaining blocker. This is the corpus at its most self-contained: one number, one relation, the whole ladder.

Verify it yourself. The complex-rung-generator ships an exact-arithmetic harness suite (the open-tag resolution check, 74/74) run on every push; see code/2026-07-complex-rung-generator.
Module 5

When Distance Lies — Transport Resistance

Transport Resistance 2 papers

What you'll learn

  • The mixed-boundary torsion problem −∇·(𝒜∇u) = q as the true governing object for a steady maintenance/transport burden — and its exact laws and sharp bounds.
  • Why distance is provably the wrong scalar: on tapered "horn" domains a distance-only estimate can overcharge by an unbounded factor.
  • The accumulated transport resistance surrogate: burden tracks demand routed through each transport cross-section, Q↓(s)/(P·A), not distance or narrowness.

The first applied branch leaves number theory for analysis, but keeps the corpus's habit of proving exactly what is forced. The mixed-boundary torsion problem −∇·(𝒜∇u) = q, with a Dirichlet supply portion and reflecting walls, is the object that actually governs whether an interior point can be maintained against a distributed demand. The "horn paper" records its forced mechanics: an exact one-dimensional law, a ratio-free ball-mean bound (constant n(n+2) = 15 in three dimensions), a sharp star-shaped lower bound, and the closure of the distance conjecture — an explicit tapered-horn family drives the coefficient P‖u‖∞/(qR²) all the way to infimum 0 (a clean rational witness, 423/2890 < 1/6), so distance to the supply set gives no universal lower bound. The straight cone k = 2 is the exact transition.

The second paper turns that into a research protocol. If distance overcharges, what is the right cheap scalar? The accumulated transport resistance says burden tracks the demand routed through each available transport cross-section, Q↓(s)/(P·A). That single ratio separates two regimes — volume-graded tapers (burden falls, ρu ≪ 1) and demand-loaded bottlenecks (burden rises, ρu = 1.83 > 1) — and a Phase-A "surrogate contest" makes any geometry-only predictor pass a gatekeeper (does it track the exact field u?) before any biological claim. The gain, on a matched-distance family, is the restriction × demand interaction, not distance alone.

Verify it yourself. Every displayed identity in the horn paper is machine-checked in exact rational arithmetic (its p2-softcheck, 28 checks); the ATR numerical claims come from its companion surrogate_contest.py (see the paper's verification section). A display erratum (the k=10 tube value is 1/22 = 0.045) is logged in ERRATA.md.
Module 6

The Catalog as a Manifold — Surprisal Geometry

Surprisal Geometry 1 paper

What you'll learn

  • How to re-read the Mahler-measure catalog Ω = {√2, √3, √5, φ, τ, φ⁴, K} as a probability family and give it a Fisher–Rao geometry.
  • The identity C(β) = β² I(β) — Fisher information is a heat capacity — and why the geometry is an exact round ¼-sphere.
  • The classification of its constant-curvature surfaces: exactly 8 at k=2, forced by a two-line determinantal master identity.

The second applied branch turns the corpus's own numbers into statistics. The catalog's Mahler measures sum to the field constant Z = 17 + 4√5, and through the description cost log M they define a Gibbs family pi(β) ∝ Mi−β — a forced exponential family. On it, surprisal is affine in cost and the Fisher information equals the cost variance; the geometry is dually flat, and its thermodynamics obey C(β) = β² I(β) — Fisher information is literally a heat capacity. Adjoin the degree as a second statistic and the Gaussian curvature is exactly the constant ¼: an isometric round quarter-sphere, embedded as a ruled surface.

The worked edition then classifies everything. The constant-¼ two-statistic surfaces are exactly eight at k = 2, and the count is forced not by search but by a two-line determinantal master identity, P = Z² · Σ|s|=4 q₄(s) ws (Sylvester's identity plus Cauchy–Binet). The full landscape at every k is a partitioned-affine classification with counts 8/56/95/31/1, and the compositum coupling is shown forced. It is the whole method in miniature: a conjecture replaced by an exact identity, every number machine-verified.

Verify it yourself. This is the most reproducible paper in the archive: 20 fail-first harnesses, 422 exact-arithmetic checks over ℚ(log2, log3, log5, log φ). Run the fast lane (ci_check.py, ~2.5 min) or the whole suite (run_all.sh) in code/2026-07-field-surprisal-geometry.
