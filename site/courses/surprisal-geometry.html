Lesson 01 · field surprisal · Z = 17 + 4√5

The Catalog as a Gibbs Family

Seven algebraic seeds, each priced by its Mahler measure. Charge that price at an inverse temperature and the catalog becomes an exponential family — with surprisal affine in cost and Fisher information equal to the cost's own variance.

12/12field_surprisal_v2.py · exact

Before any geometry, a question of pricing. A number field emits algebraic seeds, and information theory says the fair charge for an outcome is its description length. This lesson fixes that price — the Mahler measure — turns the price into a probability law with a single knob, an inverse temperature β, and then extracts the two facts the whole course runs on: surprisal is a straight line in the price, and the information the ensemble carries is exactly how much the price fluctuates. Every layer downstream is geometry read off those two facts, so it is worth meeting the seven prices first, and seeing that not one of them was chosen.

§1  One catalog, seven forced numbers

The emission catalog is the finite set Ω = {√2, √3, √5, φ, τ, φ⁴, K}. Each seed α carries a Mahler measure M(α) = ∏|ζ|>1|ζ| — the product of the conjugate root moduli that escape the unit circle — which the companion λ = 2c manuscript reads as a description cost log M. Every number below is re-derived from the minimal polynomial:

§2  The Mahler–Gibbs family

Charge the cost Ei = log Mi at inverse temperature β; the maximum-entropy law is the Mahler–Gibbs family

§3 · the family pi(β) = Mi−β / Z(β),    Z(β) = ∑j Mj−β,    A(β) := log Z(β) An exponential family with sufficient statistic log M. Its structure is forced; only the value of β is declared. Marked temperatures: β = 0 (uniform); β = 1 (the MDL/Occam prior p ∝ 1/M); β = λ = √5 (the field's forced exchange rate); β = −1 (the exact anchor p ∝ M, algebraic in ℚ(√5)).

Intuitively β is a dial on how sharply description cost is punished. At β = 0 the dial is off and every seed is equally likely; turn it up and the cheap-to-describe seeds crowd out the rest; turn it negative and the law rewards the expensive seeds instead. The same seven numbers, re-weighted along a single one-parameter curve — that curve is the whole object the rest of the course measures. Nothing else varies; the seeds and their prices are fixed, and β slides the ensemble along them.

§3  Surprisal is affine in cost

The surprisal of an outcome is its description length Si = −log pi. On the Gibbs family it is exactly a straight line in the cost:

Prop · affinity Si(β) = −log pi(β) = β·log Mi + log Z(β)   ⇒   ∂Si/∂(log Mi) = β The slope of surprisal against Mahler cost is the inverse temperature forced. At β = λ = √5 the surprisal equals the growth-rule cost λ·log M up to the shared constant log Z, tying this geometry back to the exchange rate.

Read plainly: "surprise" and "cost" are the same axis, only rescaled by β and shifted by log Z. A seed that costs one extra nat to describe is exactly β nats more surprising — no more, no less. That is why the single knob β does so much work: it is literally the exchange rate between description cost and surprise, and the next section shows its derivative controls how informative the ensemble is.

§4  Fisher information is cost variance

Differentiate the log-partition twice. The first derivative is the (negative) mean cost, the second is the load-bearing identity of the whole course:

Prop · Fisher = Var A′(β) = Z′/Z = −∑j pj log Mj = −𝔼β[log M]   ·   I(β) = A″(β) = Varβ(log M) ≥ 0 The second derivative of an exponential family's log-partition is the variance of its sufficient statistic; non-negativity is a variance's forced. So information is cost fluctuation — the more the description costs scatter at a temperature, the more the ensemble carries.

Read through the Cramér–Rao bound established, this says the temperature of an emission ensemble is easiest to estimate exactly where the costs fluctuate most: for n independent emissions, Var(β̂) ≥ 1/(n·I(β)) = 1/(n·Varβ(log M)). Cost variance is not noise here; it is the signal.

§5  Two concrete slices

Work one temperature explicitly. The exact anchor β = −1 sets pi = Mi/Z with Z = 17 + 4√5 ≈ 25.944: the costliest seed φ⁴ has M = (7+3√5)/2 ≈ 6.854, so p(φ⁴) = 6.854/25.944 ≈ 0.264, and running the same division down the catalog gives the seven anchor weights (0.077, 0.116, 0.193, 0.062, 0.062, 0.264, 0.226) — all algebraic in ℚ(√5), summing to 1. So β = −1 favours the high-Mahler seeds φ⁴, K and most suppresses the golden pair φ, τ (equal weight 0.062, since M(φ) = M(τ) = φ). And the keystone sub-catalog {Rn} — powers of the Fibonacci companion matrix, with M(Rn) = φn — has cost linear in n, so its Gibbs law is exactly a shifted geometric distribution pn = (1−r)rn−n₀ with r = φ−β and I(β) = (log φ)²·r/(1−r)² forced. The row n = 4 carries the catalog's gap seed x²−7x+1, whose trace L₄ = 7 is the normalisation that recurs across the corpus.

Lesson 02 · dual flatness · the thermodynamic identity

Dual Flatness and the Heat Capacity

The one convex potential A = log Z carries two flat coordinate systems and a divergence. Reading β as an inverse temperature makes the whole apparatus a thermodynamics — in which Fisher information is the heat capacity.

carries forward
12/12field_surprisal_v2.py · dual-flat + thermo

Lesson 1 pulled two very different things out of a single function. Differentiate A = log Z once and you get the mean cost; differentiate it twice and you get the Fisher information — a bookkeeping constant that quietly generates the statistics of the whole family. This lesson explains why. A is a convex potential, and a convex potential never travels alone: it drags a second coordinate system, a notion of divergence, and — once we agree to read β as an inverse temperature — an entire thermodynamics behind it. The payoff is a single identity, C(β) = β²·I(β): the same cost-variance that measured information in Lesson 1 measures heat here.

§1  One potential, two flat coordinates

A coordinate is flat when the family's natural straight lines — its geodesics for a chosen connection — really are straight lines in that coordinate. An exponential family always has two such coordinates, dual to each other, and A = log Z is the hinge between them. In the sense of Amari–Nagaoka established the Mahler–Gibbs family is dually flat: the natural (e-affine) coordinate is β itself; the expectation (m-affine) coordinate is η = A′(β) = −U(β), where U(β) = 𝔼β[log M] is the mean description cost; and the two dual potentials are A(β) = log Z and its Legendre transform, the negentropy A*(η) = −H(p).

The picture to hold is Legendre duality — the same move that trades a function for its slope, or position for momentum. β indexes the family by temperature; η indexes the very same points by their mean cost. Because A is strictly convex — its second derivative I(β) is a variance, hence positive — the map β ↦ η is a strictly monotone change of coordinate, so nothing is lost passing between them. But a curve straight in one coordinate need not be straight in the other, and that mismatch is the entire source of the geometry Lesson 3 will measure.

§2  KL is a Bregman divergence of log Z

How far apart are two temperatures? The honest information-theoretic answer is the Kullback–Leibler divergence — the expected number of extra nats you pay to describe emissions drawn from p(β₁) using a codebook tuned to p(β₂). On a dually flat family that divergence has a closed form: it is exactly the Bregman divergence of the convex potential A.

Prop · Bregman KL KL(p(β₁) ‖ p(β₂)) = A(β₂) − A(β₁) + (β₂ − β₁)·U(β₁) Exactly the Bregman divergence generated by the convex potential A, and the Legendre identity A*(η) = βη − A(β) = ∑ pi log pi = −H holds identically forced. Consequence: the Gibbs curve β ↦ p(β) is an e-geodesic — a straight line in the natural coordinate.

A Bregman divergence measures exactly one thing: the gap between a convex curve and its own tangent line. Stand at β₁ and draw the tangent to A there; the divergence to β₂ is how far the true value A(β₂) sits above that tangent. Strict convexity makes the gap positive whenever β₂ ≠ β₁ and zero only when they coincide — which is why KL, though not a genuine metric, behaves like a squared distance near the diagonal.

Worked, reading A and U from the thermodynamic table of §4: KL(p(−1) ‖ p(1)) = A(1) − A(−1) + 2U(−1) = 0.950 − 3.256 + 2(1.458) = 0.610 nats computed — the description-length gap between the exact anchor and the MDL prior, and summing p(−1)·log[p(−1)/p(1)] directly over the seven seeds returns the same 0.610. Because the geometry is dually flat, the generalized Pythagorean theorem also holds: if the e-geodesic from p to q meets the m-geodesic from q to r Fisher-orthogonally, then KL(p‖r) = KL(p‖q) + KL(q‖r) — a right-triangle law for information, with KL in the role of squared length.

§3  The catalog as a thermodynamics

Nothing so far has named a temperature; the name is a choice, but a productive one. Read the cost as an energy, Ei = log Mi, and β as an inverse temperature T = 1/β, and every classical potential of statistical mechanics is already sitting inside Lesson 1's objects: free energy F = −A/β, internal energy U = −A′, and Gibbs entropy S = A + βU, which is exactly the Shannon entropy H(p(β)). The dictionary is not an analogy bolted on afterward — the Gibbs law is the Boltzmann distribution of these energies, so the thermodynamic identities are theorems, not metaphors.

Two limits anchor the intuition. As β → +∞ (cooling toward T = 0) the measure freezes onto the cheapest seeds, the golden pair φ, τ; as β → −∞ it concentrates on the costliest, φ⁴. Disorder is greatest in between, at the infinite-temperature point β = 0, where the law is uniform and S = log 7 ≈ 1.946 — the most entropy seven seeds can carry.

§4  Fisher information is a heat capacity

Heat capacity asks a physical question: warm the ensemble by one degree — how much energy does it soak up? In statistical mechanics the answer is always an energy fluctuation, and here the energy is the description cost, whose fluctuation is precisely Lesson 1's Fisher information. The two notions are one variance, read through the temperature:

Prop · C = β²I C(β) = dU/dT = β²·I(β) = β²·Varβ(log M) ≥ 0 dU/dβ = −A″ = −I and dT/dβ = −1/β², so C = (dU/dβ)/(dT/dβ) = β²I forced. The same variance that measures information also measures how the ensemble's energy responds to warming: information is heat capacity.

It pays to read one entry by hand. At β = √5 the cost fluctuation is I(√5) = Var√5(log M) = 0.10405, so C = β²·I = 5 × 0.10405 = 0.520 — the whole heat capacity is the cost variance scaled by β². Now compare β = −1, where a larger fluctuation 0.2626 yields a smaller C = 0.2626 because there β² = 1: warming is always measured against T = 1/β, so the same jitter of costs buys more or less stored heat depending on how cold the ensemble already is.

§5  Where information and heat part ways

C and I are built from one variance, yet they peak at different places, and that gap is the most instructive fact of the lesson. The bare fluctuation Varβ(log M) is largest at a distinguished peak-fluctuation temperature β* ≈ −0.0768, where Var ≈ 0.3289 computed — barely above the value 0.3283 it takes at the uniform point β = 0. But the heat capacity C = β²·I carries the extra β² weight, so it is crushed to zero at β = 0 and grows with |β|. Information is maximal where the ensemble is most uncertain; heat capacity is suppressed there, because at infinite temperature the populations are already flat and hardly shift when warmed. The one variance wears two hats, and the β² factor is what tells them apart — the crossing the next lesson will hear as sub-additive distances.

answer

At β = 0 every seed has weight 1/7, so we just take unweighted moments of the seven costs log M = (0.6931, 1.0986, 1.6094, 0.4812, 0.4812, 1.9248, 1.7671). Their mean is U(0) = 8.055/7 = 1.1508. Yet C(0) = β²·I(0) = 0²·0.3283 = 0. The costs fluctuate maximally at β = 0, but the temperature there is infinite, and warming an already-uniform ensemble stores no heat per degree. The β² prefactor is the entire difference between the information reading and the heat reading of the one variance.

Q2The Gibbs curve is an e-geodesic — a straight line in β. Why does that not make it a shortest path in the Fisher–Rao metric?
answer

e-geodesics are straight in the natural (dual-flat) coordinate; metric geodesics are straight in the Fisher–Rao Riemannian connection. Dual flatness gives two flat connections that are not the metric one, so the e-geodesic and the metric geodesic are different curves. Lesson 3 makes this visible: the Fisher–Rao distances along the Gibbs curve are strictly sub-additive, so the straight-in-β curve is a longer arc than the metric chord between its endpoints.

Q3Write KL(p(β₁)‖p(β₂)) as a Bregman divergence and use it to explain in one line why KL is not symmetric.
answer

KL(p(β₁)‖p(β₂)) = A(β₂) − A(β₁) + (β₂−β₁)U(β₁): the gap between A at β₂ and its first-order Taylor expansion about β₁, i.e. the Bregman divergence of the convex A. It is asymmetric because the linearization is taken at β₁ — swapping the arguments linearizes at β₂ instead, and a strictly convex A gives a different remainder. The worked value KL(p(−1)‖p(1)) = 0.610 nats is one such one-sided gap.

Q4The cost fluctuation Varβ(log M) peaks not at the uniform point β = 0 but slightly off it, at β* ≈ −0.0768. What singles out β*, and how does the heat capacity behave there?
answer

Differentiate the fluctuation: I′(β) = −κ₃(β), minus the third cumulant — the skew — of the description cost forced. So the fluctuation is stationary exactly where the cost distribution is momentarily unskewed, κ₃ = 0; that point is β* ≈ −0.0768, with Var ≈ 0.3289, a hair above the 0.3283 at β = 0. The peak sits just negative of uniform because the seven costs are mildly asymmetric.

The heat capacity ignores this peak entirely: C = β²·I is near-zero across the whole neighbourhood of 0 (C(β*) ≈ 0.0019), because the β² factor dominates there. Maximal information with negligible heat is the sharpest form of the two readings coming apart — and it is where Lesson 3's max-Fisher principle will place one candidate temperature.

The Round ¼-Sphere

Square-root the probabilities and the categorical model is an isometric quarter-sphere: curvature exactly ¼, distances are Bhattacharyya angles, the seeds a regular simplex. The one declared quantity — the temperature — is then proved to be irreducibly a choice.

Lessons 1–2 measured the family one number at a time — a variance, a heat capacity. But a variance is a local quantity: it reports how the family responds to an infinitesimal nudge, not what shape it has in the large. This lesson supplies the shape. A single change of coordinate — take the square root of every probability — turns the Fisher–Rao geometry into a patch of an ordinary round sphere, where "distance" becomes an angle you can picture and every earlier quantity acquires a geometric meaning. It then spends that picture on a sharp question: is the temperature β, the one thing we were allowed to choose, secretly forced after all?

§1  The categorical model is a sphere

On the interior of the simplex Δm−1 the Fisher–Rao metric is gij = δij/pi. The square-root embedding trivializes it:

Lemma · the sphere estab Φ(p) = 2√p  ⇒  ∑i(d·2√pi)² = ∑i pi−1 dpi² = g,    ∑i(2√pi)² = 4  ⇒  radius 2, curvature ¼ An isometry onto the positive orthant of the sphere Sm−1(2); a radius-r sphere has constant sectional curvature 1/r² = ¼. This layer is C. R. Rao's, not the corpus's — the square-root embedding and its round metric are classical (Bull. Calcutta Math. Soc. 37 (1945), 81–91). What is corpus content is that this seven-seed catalog forms the family at all, and everything downstream.

Why the square root? The Fisher–Rao metric weights each coordinate by 1/pi, which blows up as a probability nears zero — the geometry is badly stretched near the edges of the simplex. The map p ↦ 2√p is precisely the substitution that cancels that stretch: d(2√p) = p−1/2dp, so squared lengths collapse into flat Euclidean sums, while the constraint ∑p = 1 becomes ∑(2√p)² = 4 — the equation of a sphere of radius 2. A radius-r sphere has curvature 1/r², here 1/2² = ¼, and that single line is the entire origin of the number ¼.

Every object of Lessons 1–2 now lives on a fixed curvature-¼ sphere, and the Gibbs curve's length element is √I(β) = √Varβ(log M).

§2  Distances are Bhattacharyya angles

Prop · distance dFR(p, q) = 2·arccos ∑i √(pi qi)   ·   dFR(δi, δj) = 2·arccos 0 = π Geodesic distance is the Bhattacharyya angle; the seven pure states are mutually equidistant at π, a regular spherical simplex forced. The information length of the Gibbs curve is L(β₀,β₁) = ∫ √I(β) dβ.

The quantity ∑√(piqi) is the Bhattacharyya coefficient, the overlap between two distributions — 1 when they coincide, 0 when their supports are disjoint. On the sphere it is a cosine: the two square-root vectors sit at a central angle whose cosine is exactly that overlap, and the geodesic distance is twice the angle (twice, because the radius is 2). So distinguishability is angle — two laws that barely overlap sit almost antipodally, and two pure seeds, sharing no support, sit a full quarter-turn apart, at distance π.

§3  The temperature is a coordinate, not a parameter

Lesson 1 left β as the family's one declared input. Half of that dissolves. Require only that the law depend on the seeds through the Mahler measure alone, pi ∝ f(Mi) with f measurable, and that it be consistent across product catalogs; that forces the form:

Thm · forced form product functoriality + measurable Cauchy  ⇒  f(x) = c·x−β Factorization on product catalogs is Cauchy's multiplicative equation f(xy) = f(x)f(y)/f(1), whose measurable solutions are the power laws forced. So the Mahler–Gibbs family is forced and β is the canonical affine coordinate of a forced e-geodesic — of finite total information length Ltot = ∫−∞∞ √I dβ ≈ 5.64622, running from the golden merge {φ, τ} (β → +∞) to the φ⁴ vertex (β → −∞).

§4  The dichotomy: the one choice stays a choice

What remains declared is only the operating point on that forced curve — and no invariance selects one. A finite-length one-dimensional Riemannian curve has isometry group {id, flip}, and the flip swaps the endpoints; but the endpoints are distinguishable (β → +∞ is uniform on the golden pair, β → −∞ is the single φ⁴ vertex), so the labeled-endpoint isometry group is trivial and symmetry selects no interior point forced. Q1Why is every pair of distinct pure states at Fisher–Rao distance exactly π, regardless of which seeds?
answer

Pure states are the standard basis vectors δi, δj with disjoint support. Their Bhattacharyya coefficient ∑k √(δi(k)δj(k)) = 0, so dFR = 2·arccos 0 = π. Under Φ(p) = 2√p they map to orthogonal points on the radius-2 sphere, a quarter-turn apart, and 2·(π/2) = π. The value is combinatorial — it depends only on disjoint support, never on the seeds' arithmetic.

§1  The correction that shaped everything

Adjoin a statistic X to a = log M: the two-parameter Gibbs family pi ∝ exp(−β₁ai − β₂Xi) has a genuine 2D Fisher metric, the Hessian ∇²A = Covβ(log M, X). §2  Sufficiency is a suspension

The degree is special: degi = 2 + 2·1{i=K}, so degree is affinely the indicator of the single outcome K. When X is a single-outcome indicator, pass to square-root coordinates — the apex mass gives a latitude ψ, the conditional family on the rest traces a unit curve x(θ), and √p = (cos ψ·x(θ), sin ψ):

The factor 4 is Lesson 3's embedding p ↦ 2√p onto the radius-2 sphere; the intrinsic curvature is 1/2² = ¼.

The embedding is a ruled surface, not a subsphere: because c² = c (idempotent) and a·c = aK·c stay in the span V, the second fundamental form has IIcc = IIac = 0 — the c-flow lines are great circles and the surface is swept by them — while a² ∉ V (three or more distinct a-values) gives IIaa ≠ 0: it bends along a. So det II ≤ 0, and every indicator surface sits at or below the ¼ ceiling, the eight families being the equality case. An apex is essential: a control with no indicator structure has non-constant, sign-changing curvature — the bundle's own controls give 4/21 (m = 4) and 857/16928 (m = 5), both ≠ ¼ forced.

Q2Why must the indicator be of a single outcome? What breaks for the 3-set indicator #{|ζ|>1}?
answer

A single-outcome indicator makes the family a spherical suspension over one apex: √p = (cos ψ·x, sin ψ), whose metric is round for any base curve x, hence curvature ¼ exactly. Q3The master identity P = Z²·∑ q₄(s) ws replaced ≈ 5000 samples. Why does linear independence of the window monomials collapse the whole necessity question?

Distinct squarefree monomials are linearly independent as polynomials in the free weights, so P ≡ 0 iff every coefficient q₄(s) = 0 — a finite, exact, window-by-window test, with no need to reason about how monomials might collide. Each q₄(s) = −∏(triple minors) then vanishes iff three of four plane points are dependent, and the plane lemma finishes it.

Lesson 4 settled two statistics — eight surfaces. But a catalog of seven seeds can be pinned by three, four, even five statistics at once, and the natural expectation is that the story only grows more tangled. It does the opposite. A single observation about the arithmetic of collisions closes the classification at every k in one stroke; the full count turns out to be a partition tally that quietly refutes the obvious conjecture; and when two fields are forced to share a compositum, the coupling that looked like a free choice in the product becomes forced — and curvature-visible.

§1  The squarefree key

At k = 2 the weights were free and the window monomials independent. On the family they are not: two windows collide when their exponents tie, and the tying directions are exactly Lesson 1's collision lattice — the rank-2 kernel spanned by the golden swap d₁ and the Salem square d₂. The resolving observation:

Thm · squarefree collisions 1s − 1s′ ∈ {−1, 0, 1}⁷,   but d₂ carries the entry −2 at K  ⇒  cube ∩ lattice = {0, ±d₁} Window collisions are squarefree, so the Salem square cannot act at window level — only the golden swap can, and it fires only when every statistic ties on {φ, τ}. If some statistic separates the pair, no two windows collide; if all tie, φ, τ are twins — swapped windows are equal minor-by-minor and twin-containing windows vanish outright forced.

Either way every window coefficient is forced individually, so necessity closes for 2 ≤ k ≤ 5: constant ¼ ⟺ every window matrix has rank ≤ 1 — the same multiplicity-2 mechanism that made the master identity's heavy cofactor exactly 1. The eight-branch machinery of the k = 2 proof is retired to an independent verification lane.

§4  The compositum couples

Two independent catalogs form a Riemannian product Sm₁−1(2) × Sm₂−1(2) — block-diagonal Fisher, zero cross-curvature — so any coupling term would be a declared warp (the Čencov argument that leaves β and the conformal scale undetermined). Over the compositum it stops being a choice. Form the tensor polynomial p ⊗ q (its roots are all conjugate products αiβj — no root chosen, so gauge-free) and the compositum cost c(p,q) = log M(p ⊗ q) is not additively separable:

Thm · the coupling is forced c(√2,√3) + c(φ,φ) − c(√2,φ) − c(φ,√3) = log 2,    c(φ,φ) + c(φ⁴,φ⁴) − 2·c(φ,φ⁴) = −6 log φ Both exact, as nonzero coefficient vectors forced. The rational sector {√2, √3, √5} stays separable; the golden tie persists (c(φ,·) = c(τ,·)); and the double-centered interaction tensor Δ is nonzero of exact rank 5. The declared warp is, in the compositum reading, a forced datum — and Δ is not determined by the charge data: (√2,√3) and (√2,√5) share the charge pair (ℤ/2, ℤ/2) yet differ in Δ.

And the coupled family genuinely curves. On the 49-cell tensor catalog the triple (cost₁, cost₂, c) spans dim V = 4 and carries a genuine 5-circuit, so it is not partitioned-affine and its window has rank ≥ 2. The Gauss obstructions at the uniform point are nonzero: sec(cost₁, cost₂) = ¼ − 0.2485 ≈ 0.0015 and sec(costi, c) = ¼ − 0.0838 ≈ 0.1662 computed — yet Covuniform(cost₁, cost₂) = 0 exactly.

The full model carries three statistics, and Cov(cost₁, c), Cov(cost₂, c) are both nonzero (exact rational forms in the four logs; ℚ-independence certifies they cannot cancel). The sharp statement: the two marginal costs remain exactly uncorrelated while the nonseparable tensor-cost statistic produces a curved, non-windowwise-flat joint family. A second-moment instrument restricted to the margins cannot see the coupling; the full second-moment matrix can, and curvature sees more still — already at the uniform point, below the reach of the cross-covariance of the margins.
