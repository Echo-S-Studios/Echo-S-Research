Lesson 01 · the exact laws

The Torsion Problem, and the Exact 1-D Law

One PDE stands in for "how hard is it to maintain an interior point against demand." Before any bound, fix the object exactly — and solve it on the line, where everything is explicit.

28/28exact checks · p2-softcheck

§1  The mixed-boundary problem

Let Ω ⊂ ℝⁿ be a bounded connected Lipschitz domain whose boundary splits into a supply portion Σ (relatively open, positive surface measure) and the reflecting remainder Γ. A measurable symmetric coefficient tensor 𝒜 is uniformly elliptic, PminI ≤ 𝒜 ≤ PmaxI a.e. The torsion function u is the unique member of V = {φ ∈ H¹(Ω) : tr φ = 0 on Σ} solving

def · weak form B[u,φ] := ∫Ω ∇φ · 𝒜∇u dz = ∫Ω q φ dz   for all φ ∈ V This weak identity encodes two boundary conditions at once: u = 0 on the supply Σ (Dirichlet), and the natural no-flux condition 𝒜∇u·ν = 0 on the reflecting Γ (Neumann). Here q > 0 is a constant volumetric demand.

Existence and uniqueness are Lax–Milgram: the Poincaré inequality on V, plus coercivity from uniform ellipticity. Crucially, Pmin enters only through well-posedness — no estimate in the whole theory depends on it. That is what "ratio-free" means below: a constant depends on Pmax alone, never on the ellipticity ratio Pmax/Pmin, the volume, or any regularity modulus.

positivity u ≥ 0 a.e., and u ≢ 0 Test the weak form with the truncation u⁻ = max(−u,0), whose trace still vanishes on Σ; the energy is ≤ 0 and ≥ 0 at once, so u⁻ ≡ 0. The maintenance cost is never negative and never trivial. forced

§2  What we are bounding: the distance conjecture

Read u(x) as the accumulated cost of maintaining the point x against demand under constrained transport; ∥u∥∞ is the worst-case burden. The tempting guess is that this is controlled from below by distance to the supply. Write dE for Euclidean distance to Σ, dΩ for the shortest path inside Ω, and d𝒜 for the operator-intrinsic distance; with R• = supΩ d•(·,Σ), the distance conjecture P2 asserted, in three nested strengths,

P2-E ⇐ P2-G ⇐ P2-A ∥u∥∞ ≥ q RE² / (2n Pmax)  ⇐  ∥u∥∞ ≥ q RΩ² / (2n Pmax)  ⇐  ∥u∥∞ ≥ q R𝒜² / (2n) Since dE ≤ dΩ ≤ √Pmax d𝒜, the variants nest: P2-A ⇒ P2-G ⇒ P2-E. Lessons 1–2 prove what does hold; Lesson 3 shows all three are false.

§3  The one-dimensional law, exactly

On the line the problem is an ODE with a Dirichlet end and a reflecting end, and it solves in closed form. Let 0 < Pmin ≤ A(x) ≤ Pmax be measurable on (0,L), q > 0:

Thm · exact 1-D law −(A u′)′ = q,  u(0) = 0,  (A u′)(L) = 0   ⟹   u(x) = q ∫₀ˣ (L − s)/A(s) ds Read off the flux directly: A(x)u′(x) = q(L − x), which is absolutely continuous, vanishes at the reflecting end x = L, and has derivative −q. Uniqueness: any difference v has (Av′)′ = 0, so Av′ is constant, and the far-end condition forces it to 0.

Because u′ = q(L − x)/A ≥ 0, u is nondecreasing, and since A ≤ Pmax with L − s ≥ x − s on [0,x],

far-end bound · equality u(x) ≥ q x² / (2 Pmax),   ∥u∥∞ = u(L) = q ∫₀ᵤ (L−s)/A(s) ds ≥ q L² / (2 Pmax) Equality in the far-end bound holds if and only if A = Pmax a.e.: the integrand (L−s)(1/A − 1/Pmax) is nonnegative and L − s > 0, so it can only vanish where A already saturates. forced

Here Σ = {0} and d(x,Σ) = x, so the bound reads u ≥ q·d(·,Σ)²/(2Pmax) — the conjectured constant 2n at n = 1. So in one dimension the distance conjecture is simply true, and sharp. The trouble starts when "distance" stops being the only thing that varies.

the fork ahead Theorem 1 is the conductivity-graded balance: a sink of q per unit length, with conductivity A(x) varying. There is a second, different one-dimensional balance — a sink of q per unit volume in a tube of varying cross-section — and it is the mechanism of Lesson 3. The conjecture above extrapolated the first balance and quietly assumed it was the only one. It is not.

Q1In the 1-D law, why does the reflecting condition sit at the far end x = L, and where does the factor (L − s) come from?
answer

The flux A u′ must carry off all the demand deposited between a point and the reflecting wall. Integrating −(Au′)′ = q from x to L with (Au′)(L) = 0 gives A(x)u′(x) = q(L − x): the flux at x equals the total sink q·(L − x) downstream of it. Dividing by A and integrating from 0 (where u = 0) gives the law. The reflecting end is what makes the flux "pile up" toward the supply.

Q2A material has A(x) dip well below Pmax on part of (0,L). Does ∥u∥∞ go up or down relative to the uniform Pmax case, and is the far-end bound still valid?
answer

Up. Smaller A means larger 1/A in u(L) = q∫(L−s)/A ds, so the burden increases. The bound ∥u∥∞ ≥ qL²/(2Pmax) stays valid — it is a floor set by the largest the conductivity is ever allowed to be — and it is met with equality only when A never dips at all.

Verify it. The 1-D flux law and its bounds are among the horn paper's 28 exact-arithmetic checks (companion p2-softcheck.py, exit 0); no numeral here is asserted un-run.

Lesson 02 · interior bounds

Interior Bounds: the Ball Mean and the Star

Two lower bounds survive in every dimension — one that asks nothing of the coefficient, one that is pointwise-sharp under a single geometric condition. Both land on the constant 2n.

carries forward
exit 0p2-softcheck · ball & star

§1  Comparison, then the ball mean

Two one-line lemmas do the work. First, same-operator domain comparison: if ω ⊆ Ω and w is the full-Dirichlet torsion of ω for the same 𝒜 and q, then u ≥ w a.e. on ω forced — shrinking the domain to full-Dirichlet only lowers the solution. Second, torsional-rigidity anti-monotonicity: a larger coefficient gives a smaller integral, ∫ω w ≥ ∫ω w′ when 𝒜 ≤ 𝒜′ forced, from the Dirichlet-energy variational principle. Together they compare any u against the explicit constant-coefficient ball solution.

Thm · ball mean bound for every ball Br(x₀) ⊆ Ω:  (1/|Br|) ∫Br u ≥ q r² / (n(n+2) Pmax),  hence ∥u∥∞ ≥ q r² / (n(n+2) Pmax) The ball's own solution for PmaxI is w(z) = q(r² − |z−x₀|²)/(2n Pmax); its radial mean of r² − |z−x₀|² is exactly 2r²/(n+2). Multiply by q/(2nPmax): the mean is q r²/(n(n+2)Pmax), and the sup dominates the mean. At n = 3 the constant n(n+2) is 15.

Only B ⊆ Ω is used — the ball may touch ∂Ω, even Σ — and 𝒜 may be any measurable uniformly elliptic tensor. Taking r = the inradius gives ∥u∥∞ ≥ q rin(Ω)²/(n(n+2)Pmax), a bound attained by the ball about the deepest interior point. This is ratio-free and mean-form; it is not pointwise. For a pointwise-sharp bound we pay one hypothesis.

§2  The star-shaped theorem

Now let 𝒜 be a constant symmetric positive-definite tensor with 𝒜 ≤ PmaxI. Fix x₀ and set the quadratic form f(z) = (z − x₀)ᵀ𝒜⁻¹(z − x₀). Two identities make it a perfect test field:

Prop · two identities 𝒜∇f = 2(z − x₀),  ∇·(𝒜∇f) = 2n,  f(z) ≥ |z − x₀|² / Pmax The divergence is a constant 2n — the same 2n as the conjecture. The last inequality is an eigenvalue computation: in an 𝒜-eigenbasis, f − |z−x₀|²/Pmax = ∑ᵢ wᵢ²(Pmax − λᵢ)/(λᵢPmax) ≥ 0.

The theorem needs the axis point to "see" the reflecting wall from the inside — the star condition:

Thm · star-shaped lower bound if (z − x₀)·ν(z) ≥ 0 for a.e. z ∈ Γ, then  u(x₀) ≥ (q/2n) infz∈Σ (z−x₀)ᵀ𝒜⁻¹(z−x₀) ≥ q dE(x₀,Σ)² / (2n Pmax) Set w = u + (q/2n)f. Gauss–Green turns the star condition into B[w,φ] ≥ 0 for φ ≥ 0; a Stampacchia truncation at the minimum supply value forces w ≥ (q/2n)infΣf, and f(x₀) = 0 delivers u(x₀). The constant 2n cannot be improved, and it is ratio-free. Any convex Ω meets the star condition at every point. forced

Sharpness is exhibited, not asserted: equality holds on the ball (Σ = ∂B), on central reflection sectors (wedges cut by hyperplanes through x₀, where the star condition holds with equality on every flat cut), and on ellipsoids {f < r²} where u = (q/2n)(r² − f) exactly. The anisotropic ellipsoid case attains the sharper first inequality, the one with 𝒜⁻¹ still inside.

§3  Distance to the full boundary always works

One corollary needs no star condition at all, only constant 𝒜, and it is the fact Lesson 3 will contrast against:

Cor · full-boundary bound for constant 𝒜 ≤ PmaxI and any Σ/Γ split:  u(x₀) ≥ q·dist(x₀, ∂Ω)² / (2n Pmax) Drop the largest ball B ⊆ Ω about x₀ and apply the star theorem to (B, Σ = ∂B): the star condition is vacuous there. Distance to the whole boundary controls u unconditionally. forced

So two distances behave very differently. Distance to ∂Ω bounds u always (constant coefficients). Distance to the supply Σ bounds u under the star condition — a sharp sufficient hypothesis. The next lesson removes it and watches the second bound fail without limit, while the first stays true.

Q1The mean bound carries the constant n(n+2) = 15 (at n = 3) but the star bound carries 2n = 6. Why is the smaller constant on the stronger (pointwise) statement not a contradiction?
answer

They bound different quantities. n(n+2) governs the average of u over a ball and asks nothing of the coefficient beyond ellipticity; 2n governs the peak value u(x₀) but only under the star condition and for constant 𝒜. A larger denominator on a weaker (mean, assumption-free) claim is exactly what you expect — the average is smaller than the peak, so it accepts a looser floor.

Q2Why does the corollary about dist(x₀, ∂Ω) escape the star condition entirely?
answer

Because on the inscribed ball the supply is the whole boundary (Σ = ∂B) and Γ is empty, so the star condition "(z−x₀)·ν ≥ 0 on Γ" is a condition on the empty set — vacuously true. The reflecting wall is what can violate the star condition; with no reflecting wall there is nothing to check.

Verify it. The ball-mean constant 2r²/(n+2) → n(n+2), the identities 𝒜∇f = 2(z−x₀) and ∇·(𝒜∇f) = 2n, and the equality evaluations are checked exactly in the horn paper's p2-softcheck.py (exit 0).

Lesson 03 · closure of the distance conjecture

When Distance Lies: the Tapered Horns

A family of narrowing horns, each solved in closed form, drives the supply-distance coefficient to zero. All three metric forms of the conjecture are false — and the straight cone marks the exact transition.

exit 0p2-softcheck · 28 checks

§1  A horn, and a field that solves it

Fix a power profile g(x) = ε h (x/h)k/2 and build the truncated horn

def · truncated horn Ωk,ε,δ = { (x,y) ∈ (δh, h) × ℝn−1 : |y| < g(x) },  Σ = {h} × {|y| < εh},  𝒜 = P·I The supply Σ is the flat wide mouth at x = h. The reflecting Γ is the tapering lateral wall |y| = g(x) plus the small end-face Fδ at x = δh. It is a bounded Lipschitz domain, so all of Lesson 1's theory applies. Write ρ = |y|.

The engine is an explicit comparison field — not the torsion function, but a smooth quadratic tuned so that its flux through the lateral wall vanishes exactly:

Prop · exact field, zero wall flux v(x,y) = a(h² − x²) − b|y|²,  a = q / (P(2 + k(n−1))),  b = ak/2 Then −PΔv = q identically; ∂νv = 0 at every point of the lateral wall (the choice b = ak/2 is exactly what cancels it, using x g′ = (k/2)g); and v peaks at the tip, sup v = v(δh,0) = a h²(1 − δ²), while on the mouth v|Σ = −b|y|² ∈ [−b(εh)², 0]. forced

A Stampacchia truncation against the reflecting wall converts the vanishing wall flux into a genuine one-sided bound on the true torsion function:

Thm · comparison 0 ≤ u ≤ v + b(εh)² a.e.,  hence  ∥u∥∞ ≤ a h²(1 − δ²) + b(εh)² The demand only leaves through the mouth; the reflecting wall carries no flux for v, so v (nudged up by the mouth's depth b(εh)²) sits above u everywhere. forced

§2  The distances are honest — and the bound still fails

On the horn the geometry is transparent: for z = (x,y), every metric to the mouth is just the axial gap, dE(z,Σ) = dΩ(z,Σ) = h − x, and d𝒜 = (h−x)/√P, so RE = RΩ = √P R𝒜 = h(1 − δ). The three conjecture variants coincide here — they must stand or fall together. Dividing the comparison bound by qR²/P:

Thm · falsification (all three metrics) Pmax∥u∥∞ / (qR²) ≤ β(k,ε,δ,n) := ( 1 − δ² + (k/2)ε² ) / ( (2 + k(n−1))(1 − δ)² ) As (ε,δ) → (0,0), β → 1/(2 + k(n−1)), which is < 1/(2n) exactly when k > 2 — since 2n < 2 + k(n−1) ⇔ k > 2. Every super-conical horn therefore breaks all three forms P2-E, P2-G, P2-A at once. forced
Cor · an exact rational witness (n = 3, k = 4) δ = 3/20, ε = 1/5:  Pmax∥u∥∞/(qR²) ≤ (1 − 9/400 + 2/25) / (10·(17/20)²) = (423/400)/(2890/400) = 423/2890 < 1/6 A bounded Lipschitz domain, constant scalar coefficient, flat Dirichlet mouth — every hypothesis of the conjecture holds, and the coefficient lands 12.2% below the conjectured sharp 1/(2n) = 1/6, with the exact gap 1/6 − 423/2890 = 88/4335. Not a limit, not an approximation: one explicit domain. computed

And the failure has no floor. Taking ε² = 1/k, δ = 1/k and letting k → ∞ sends β → 0:

Cor · the infimum is zero inf { Pmax∥u∥∞ / (qR²) } = 0,  over all admissible (Ω, Σ, 𝒜, q), in each of the three metrics — and it is not attained Strictly positive for every fixed datum (positivity, Lesson 1), yet with infimum 0. No constant c > 0 can appear in any bound ∥u∥∞ ≥ c·qR²/Pmax. forced

§3  The cone is the exact transition

Why k = 2? Along the axis point x₀ = (x∗, 0), the star integrand on the wall is (1 − k/2)g(x) + x∗g′(x). For k ≤ 2 both terms are ≥ 0, so the star condition holds and Lesson 2 applies verbatim; for k > 2 it goes negative once the horn is long enough, and the star hypothesis is genuinely lost. This is a clean dichotomy:

So within the power-law family, k = 2 is the razor's edge: the straight cone sits exactly at the conjectured constant 1/(2n) in every dimension. The star condition — a sharp sufficient hypothesis — governs everything up to the cone; beyond it, its loss is accompanied by unbounded failure. (Necessity of the star condition across all geometries is not claimed.)

§4  The mechanism: it was the wrong 1-D balance

The conjecture extrapolated Lesson 1's conductivity-graded balance (sink per length). The horns realize the other one — sink per unit volume in a tube of cross-section 𝔄(s) = κn−1 g(s)n−1 — and the comparison field's tip value reproduces that graded integral identically:

the graded-resistance identity (q/P) ∫₀ᵤ V(s)/𝔄(s) ds = (q/P) ∫₀ᵤ s / (1 + k(n−1)/2) ds = q h² / (P(2 + k(n−1))) = v(0,0) The conjectured functional q d(·,Σ)²/(2n Pmax) kept the distance but threw away the volume grading V/𝔄, with V(s) = ∫₀ˢ 𝔄. The horns are exactly the geometries on which the two functionals separate without bound — distance overcharging for length the narrowing tube never has to serve.
honest caveat · the untruncated cusp A sharper cusp version (δ = 0) pushes the same bound to Pmax∥u∥∞/(qR²) ≤ (1 + (k/2)ε²)/(2 + k(n−1)), below 1/(2n) when ε² < (n−1)(k−2)/(nk). It is logically auxiliary: every closure statement above rests on the truncated Lipschitz family. The cusp uses a nonstandard boundary realization at the tip and is flagged in the paper for independent functional-analytic review — so the headline never leans on it.

Q1Lesson 2 said dist(x₀,∂Ω) bounds u unconditionally. Doesn't the horn, where u is tiny, contradict that?
answer

No — the two bounds measure different distances. On a horn, dist(z, ∂Ω) ≤ g(x) ≤ εh: the nearest boundary is the lateral wall, which is very close. So the unconditional bound only asserts u ≳ q(εh)²/(2nPmax), a tiny amount, fully consistent with the small true value. What fails is the bound using distance to the far-away supply Σ. The horn separates "distance to any boundary" from "distance to the supply."

Q2If the wall is made Dirichlet (supply) instead of reflecting, does the falsification survive?
answer

No, and that is the point. A reflecting wall is what forces demand to travel the length of the horn to the mouth. Make the wall Dirichlet and it joins Σ; every interior point is then within O(εh) of supply, all distances to supply collapse, and no conflict can arise. The closure of the conjecture requires the wall to reflect.

Q3Why does the exact witness 423/2890 matter more than the infimum-zero statement?
answer

The infimum-zero corollary is a limit — a sceptic can ask whether any single legitimate domain really violates the bound. 423/2890 is that domain: n = 3, k = 4, δ = 3/20, ε = 1/5, one bounded Lipschitz horn with a flat Dirichlet mouth, coefficient a certified 12.2% under 1/6, checked in exact rational arithmetic. It converts an asymptotic story into a finished counterexample.

Verify it. The horn solution and wall-flux identities, the coefficient β and its windows, the instance 423/2890 < 1/6, the star deficit, and the cone marginality 1/(2+k(n−1))|k=2 = 1/(2n) are among the horn paper's 28 independent exact checks (p2-softcheck.py, exit 0). Finite-volume corroboration exists but is deliberately excluded — the paper carries forced material only.

Lesson 04 · accumulated transport resistance

Accumulated Transport Resistance

The corrected scalar is not distance but demand routed through each cross-section. Naming it precisely means keeping two tube integrals apart — and refusing to call any cheap surrogate the truth until it passes a gate.

carries forward
exit 0surrogate_contest · ΔC = qℝ

§1  The thesis, and the direction of the horn result

The horn paper's lesson is easy to misread. It is not "narrowing raises resistance." It is that a distance-only readout overestimates: as a horn narrows the true burden shrinks, while distance keeps charging for the length. The corrected statement, which this paper is built around:

the thesis Transport burden depends on how much maintenance demand must pass through each available transport cross-section — Q↓(s)/(P(s)𝔄(s)) — not on distance or narrowness in isolation.

The comparison field v of Lesson 3 is recalled, not reproved: it solves −PΔv = q with exactly zero lateral-wall flux, giving the rigorous upper bound 0 ≤ u ≤ v + b(εh)². Its apex obeys the graded identity, and reading that identity in k is the whole picture:

the tube quantity shrinks; the failure factor grows (q/P) ∫₀ᵤ V(s)/𝔄(s) ds = q h² / (P(2 + k(n−1)))  —  0.167 (k=2) → 0.045 (k=10) The tube quantity decreases monotonically in the taper exponent k (at n = 3, from 1/6 to 1/22). The object that grows without bound is not the burden but the distance-only failure factor qR²/∥u∥∞, climbing from about 6 to about 22 across the same range. Narrowing lowers burden and raises the distance error together.

Whether the true apex u(0) equals this graded integral, rather than merely being bounded by it, is open. The exact, path-free, general-domain object is instead the Green representation u(x) = ∫Ω G(x,y) q(y) dy, valid wherever the mixed Green function exists forced (a.e. x; pointwise under Phase-A regularity).

§2  Two tube objects, not multiplied by demand twice

A unit ambiguity haunted earlier drafts. There are two genuinely different one-dimensional tube integrals, and only one already contains demand.

geometric resistance vs predicted drop 𝓡geom = ∫ V(s)/(P(s)𝔄(s)) ds  (declared, geometry only)
ΔCtube = ∫ Q↓(s)/(P(s)𝔄(s)) ds,  Q↓(s) = ∫downstream of s q  (declared, already carries demand) Q↓(s) is the demand still to be served beyond cross-section s. So ΔCtube already is a predicted concentration drop; 𝓡geom is pure geometry. They coincide only under uniform demand, where Q↓(s) = q V(s).
the unit split, in one line ΔCtube = q · 𝓡geom  (uniform q only) This is the entire content of the split: multiplying the demand-weighted ΔCtube by a further factor of q would double-count the load. Keeping the volume-weighted 𝓡geom and the demand-weighted ΔCtube as distinct named objects removes the ambiguity at the source. forced under uniform demand.

A canonical normalized burden 𝓡G(x) = u(x)/Q∗ is meaningful only once the normalization Q∗ is preregistered declared; for uniform demand the clean choice is Q∗ = q, giving 𝓡G = u/q. A specimen-specific Q∗ chosen after the fact would wreck any cross-specimen comparison.

§3  Three tiers, and a gatekeeper

The exact field u is the ground truth but needs a full PDE solve — precisely what a cheap predictor is meant to avoid. So the program lives on three tiers, and the whole scientific question is whether the bottom can be made to track the top:

Until that tracking is shown across synthetic geometries with known u, the honest register is "we are testing a computational surrogate inspired by the exact horn functional" — never "we have transported the horn theorem into tissue."

The caution is earned by the flux geometry. Because ∇·J = q, the current is not divergence-free: throughput varies along a tube, equal-throughput tubes need not stay equal, a global tiling can fail, and the streamlines run from the interior toward Σ. Any reduction of the field to flux tubes is an extra modelling move, not something the theorem forces.

One cheap candidate is eikonal descent — minimum-cost paths to Σ, |∇T| = 1/P. These are not flux lines, and the gap is measurable and regime-dependent:

Q1Why is ΔCtube = q·𝓡geom flagged "uniform q only"? What breaks otherwise?
answer

𝓡geom weights each cross-section by the upstream volume V(s); ΔCtube weights it by the downstream demand Q↓(s). Those agree only when demand is uniform, Q↓(s) = qV(s). With demand concentrated somewhere, Q↓ ≠ qV and the two integrals genuinely differ; writing ΔCtube = q𝓡geom then multiplies the already-demand-weighted drop by demand a second time — a double count.

Q2Both the true field u and the flux-tube diagnostic sit above the deployable surrogate. If the diagnostic is not cheap either, why keep it in the ladder?
answer

It is the reference with no path-reduction error by construction — it is what an ideal reduction of the true current would retain. A cheap surrogate is scored against it (and against u) to separate "the surrogate is a bad predictor" from "path-reduction itself loses the signal." Two Regimes, and the Surrogate Contest

One ratio splits the world into tapers and bottlenecks. A gated contest then asks which cheap summary tracks the exact burden — and answers it before a single biological claim is licensed.

§1  Two regimes, one ratio

The corrected mathematics is a genuine dichotomy, and both halves are numerically real. The organizing quantity is the local ratio Q↓(s)/(P(s)𝔄(s)) — demand routed through each cross-section:

A narrow section is cheap when little demand lies beyond it; a modest bottleneck is severe when it must serve a large downstream region. The existence of the ρu > 1 regime is not hypothetical — a bottleneck matched to its Krogh reference on maximum distance, total demand, and volume reaches ρu = 1.83 forced. Its achievable magnitude, and whether a closed-form bottleneck family exists, are the sharpest open targets.

Two amplification ratios are kept strictly apart, because one word spanning both would mislead: the true-field ρu = ∥u∥∞/uKrogh, and the surrogate ρ̂ATR = 𝓡surrATR/𝓡surrATR,Krogh. Phase A asks precisely whether ρ̂ predicts ρu.

§2  The ablation ladder names the mechanism

Reporting only a surrogate's final score would hide which ingredient produced it. So every surrogate is run up a fixed ladder that adds one information source at a time — Euclidean distance, then geodesic, then restriction (cross-section), then instead demand, then the full restriction×demand product integrated along the path, then the exact PDE burden u as ceiling. On a 16-geometry family (8 tapers, 8 bottlenecks), all matched on maximum distance, the decomposition is stark:

The ladder is genuinely decidable: the largest adjacent-rung collinearity is |corr| = 0.369, far below the ≈0.98 that would signal redundant rungs, so each rung carries independent information.

§3  Comparing models, and why correlation is not enough

The eventual biological test (Phase C) is organized around four models, and the comparison discipline is the point:

four models M₀: outcome ~ distance ·  M₁: + demand + diffusivity + volume + covariates ·  M₂: M₁ + 𝓡surrATR ·  M₃: M₁ + 𝓡G (exact-u) M₁ vs M₂ and M₁ vs M₃ are nested — likelihood-ratio tests, grouped-CV ΔR², AIC/BIC. But M₂ vs M₃ pits a surrogate against the exact burden and is generally non-nested: grouped cross-validation or Vuong-type tests, never a likelihood ratio. "Surrogate parity" is declared only when M₂ falls within a preregistered tolerance of M₃ (e.g. ΔR²CV ≤ 0.02).

This separates failure modes a single test would confound: if M₃ > M₁ but M₂ ≯ M₁, burden matters and the surrogate failed; if neither beats M₁, modelled burden adds nothing there; if M₂ ≈ M₃ > M₁, burden matters and the surrogate captures it — the target.

why a high correlation cannot be the test A surrogate with Pearson correlation 0.995 against u nonetheless reverses the ordering of 20 matched-distance pairs. So the acceptance package is not a correlation but the full set — cross-validated R², median relative error, Spearman, calibration slope and intercept, out-of-sample behaviour under severe bottlenecks, and above all pairwise-ordering preservation at matched distance. The surrogate must beat maximum distance on geometries built so the two disagree.

§4  What it means, with free-energy demoted

The empirical content stands with the free-energy principle discarded: FEP is at most an optional interface language, it does not generate the transport functional, and the explanatory arrow stays physical — geometry sets the PDE burden 𝓡G = u/Q∗, which sets maintenance feasibility. What survives, and is measurable, is a reading of u as an accessibility-cost potential, with 𝓡ATR its pathwise accumulation: 𝓡ATR rising is accessibility falling.

the principle that survives Form does not respond to raw distance. It responds to the accumulated difficulty of maintaining relation across its own geometry. Unlike the FEP bridge, this asserts no upstream awareness or optimization — only a measurable middle layer from relational geometry to biological architecture. declared

answer

Objection: 0.995 correlation still inverted 20 matched-distance pairs in a direct construction — and pair ordering at matched distance is exactly the comparison the whole program exists to get right. Q2Why must M₂ vs M₃ not be compared by a likelihood-ratio test?
answer

Because a likelihood-ratio test requires nested models — one a special case of the other. M₂ (M₁ + the surrogate) and M₃ (M₁ + the exact-u summary) are two different additions to M₁, generally non-nested. They must be compared out-of-sample: grouped cross-validation, information criteria, or Vuong-type tests. "Parity" is then a preregistered tolerance, not a judgment call.

Q3Why is the lacuno-canalicular network of bone named as the strongest test and explicitly forbidden as the first one?
answer

Its restricted cross-section departs most sharply from Krogh distance, so it is where distance and ATR most disagree — the strongest discriminator. For exactly that reason it must not be first: an LCN result means something only after the surrogate has passed the gatekeeper on synthetic horns and branching networks where the true u is known. Spheroids (ρu ≈ 1) are the principled near-Krogh negative control; muscle, where facilitated transport can negate diffusion distance, is a stress test both ATR and Krogh may fail.
