The Two Walls

Mahler, Salem & Lehmer. Two walls — a golden height floor and a four-direction angle lattice — box every Salem number out of what one construction can emit, sidestepping Lehmer's ninety-year-old problem without pretending to resolve it.

Lehmer's problem asks whether the Mahler measure of an integer polynomial can crowd arbitrarily close to 1 from above. It is open since 1933. This course does not attack it. It teaches the corpus's move instead: stop bounding all integer polynomials, build a finitely generated emission algebra, and prove exactly what that machine cannot make. The seven source papers below are its anatomy — the walls, the empty slot the walls create, what that emptiness generates, who is turned away at the door, and how far the same census reaches.

Every load-bearing claim carries a tag naming how strongly it is held — forced proved in the exact structure, computed exhaustive over a stated finite range, declared a principled or interpretive choice, plausible evidence short of proof, open unresolved, established imported from the literature. The tags are not decoration — they are the mathematics. No approximation crosses a decision boundary anywhere in the corpus: every equality, divisibility, and inequality is settled over ℚ, ℚ(√5), or ℚ(51/4).

Lesson 01 · Mahler, Salem & Lehmer

The Measure, and Lehmer's Problem

One definition, one floor that is completely understood, one open problem — and then the pivot the whole corpus descends from: don't solve it, route around it.

exactSturm-decided over ℚ · no float crosses a decision

Reads from Lehmer's Box and The Emission–Gap Theorem.

Everything in this course descends from one number and one refusal. The number is Lehmer's, the smallest Mahler measure above 1 anyone has ever exhibited, unbeaten since 1933; whether measures can creep still closer to 1 is Lehmer's problem, open ever since. The refusal is the corpus's: it declines to attack that problem. Instead it builds a specific machine, proves exactly what the machine cannot make, and shows Lehmer's number is simply not among the machine's outputs — leaving the open problem untouched. This lesson lays down the one definition everything rests on and states the pivot precisely.

§1  One definition, three values

For a nonzero p(x) = ad∏(x − αi) ∈ ℤ[x] the Mahler measure is the leading coefficient times the product of the root moduli that escape the unit circle:

def · Jensen M(p) = |ad| ∏i max(1, |αi|) = exp( ∫₀¹ log |p(e2πiθ)| dθ ) The two forms agree by Jensen's formula. Only roots strictly outside the circle contribute; everything on or inside costs a factor of exactly 1.

The measure has a clean meaning: it is how much a polynomial's roots expand, counting only the ones outside the unit circle and ignoring the rest. Roots on or inside the circle are "free" (factor 1); each root of modulus r > 1 multiplies the measure by r. Hold onto the middle row: only one root escapes, so M is that single root, and the escaping root is the golden ratio φ. That polynomial x² − x − 1 — the golden seed — is the keystone the entire construction is built on, and its measure φ will turn out to be the smallest growth the machine can ever produce.

§2  The floor at 1, completely understood

Every factor max(1, |αi|) is ≥ 1, so M ≥ 1 always — the measure has a hard floor at 1. The question is what sits exactly on that floor, and here the answer is complete and classical. Kronecker (1857) established settles equality exactly: M(p) = 1 iff every root is 0 or a root of unity — i.e. p is a power of x times cyclotomic factors. So M = 1 is the trivial, fully classified locus: nothing interesting lives on the floor itself. Every open question lives strictly above 1, and the whole drama is about how close to 1 a measure can get while staying above it.

§3  Smyth's split, and Lehmer's question

A polynomial is reciprocal if its roots are closed under α ↦ α⁻¹ (i.e. xdeg p p(1/x) = p(x)). The whole difficulty concentrates on one side of this split. Smyth (1971) established: every non-reciprocal monic integer p with M > 1 satisfies

Smyth 1971 M(p) ≥ μS = 1.3247179572…  (the plastic number, root of x³ − x − 1) μS is the smallest Pisot number. Smyth closes the non-reciprocal side outright — the trivial and the non-reciprocal cases are both settled. Everything hard is reciprocal.

Lehmer (1933) open asks the reciprocal question: is there a constant c > 1 with M(p) ≥ c whenever M(p) > 1? Or can measures crowd down onto 1? His record-holder is unbeaten after ninety years. Rather than quote its properties we derive them by the house method — the exact fold the papers use everywhere:

C2 · reciprocal fold L(x) = x¹⁰ + x⁹ − x⁷ − x⁶ − x⁵ − x⁴ − x³ + x + 1,  M(L) = 1.1762808182… Reciprocity is an identity: x¹⁰L(1/x) = L(x). Because L is reciprocal of even degree, the substitution t = z + 1/z folds it to the degree-5 trace-down Q(t) = t⁵ + t⁴ − 5t³ − 5t² + 4t + 3. On |z|=1 we have t = 2cos ψ ∈ [−2, 2]; a Sturm count over ℚ gives Q five real roots — 4 inside (−2, 2), 1 in (2, ∞). Unfolding: 8 conjugates on the circle, one real pair (β, 1/β). Exactly one root outside, so M(L) = β.

With an independent irreducibility check, L is a Salem polynomial: irreducible, reciprocal, one root β > 1, one root 1/β, all others on the circle. A Salem number is such a β — a real algebraic integer > 1 whose conjugates lie in the closed unit disk with at least one on it, minimal polynomial reciprocal of even degree 2m ≥ 4. Contrast a Pisot number: one root outside, all others strictly inside. Lehmer's number is the smallest known Salem number, and it sits at 1.176 — strictly below Smyth's μS. The canonical example and the canonical obstruction are the same object.

Dobrowolski (1979) established gives M(p) > 1 + c(log log n / log n)³, but this decays to 1 with the degree n, so it yields no uniform floor. Ninety years, no c.

§4  The pivot: don't solve it, route around it

Here is the move the whole corpus descends from. A growth rule charges a description cost of λ log M(θ) to adjoin an algebraic direction θ; that cost stays positive iff M is bounded away from 1 — but only over the directions the construction can actually emit. That last clause is the escape hatch. Bounding M over all of ℤ[x] is Lehmer's problem, ninety years unmoved. Bounding it over one finitely generated system — a fixed set of seeds closed under a fixed set of operators — is a different, tractable question, and the entire strategy is to prove one closure statement about that system:

the strategy no Salem number lies in the emission image. With Smyth, this alone forces the cost floor: everything in the band (1, μS) is a reciprocal Salem-type number, so excluding Salems empties the band. This is a closure property of one specific algebra — not a universal height bound — so it never touches Lehmer's problem. The papers call it "sidestepping Lehmer without resolving it," and that phrasing is load-bearing.

Why does excluding Salems suffice? Because Smyth already floored the non-reciprocal case at μS, and the only numbers that could occupy the leftover band (1, μS) are reciprocal Salem-type numbers. Knock those out of the emission and the whole band is empty from the machine's point of view — its emitted measures are pushed up to μS at least, realized at φ. The 90-year-old problem is not solved; it is made irrelevant to the machine.

§5  Two walls, and the discipline of the sidestep

The next two lessons build the two walls that enforce the closure: a height wall at φ (Lesson 2), which fences off small measures, and an angle wall at the four directions (π/2)ℤ (Lesson 3), which forbids Salem-type on-circle conjugates outright. Together they box every Salem number out of the emission image.

What must stay exactly straight is the logical status of the result. Call it (B): "the emission image avoids the Salem band." Lehmer's conjecture (C) — that the true infimum is Lehmer's number — would imply (B), so (B) is strictly weaker than the open problem, and far narrower in scope (one algebra, not all of ℤ[x]). Crucially, (B) is proved unconditionally, and it stays true whether Lehmer's problem turns out true or false: if small Salems exist, they still violate a wall and are still not emitted. So (B) neither assumes nor settles Lehmer. Lehmer's number is excluded as an emission, never as a number; the open problem is left exactly where it stood. That is what "sidestepping without resolving" means, and keeping the registers separate is the discipline the whole course runs on.

The Height Wall — the Golden Floor

The first wall is a fence on the height axis: the open strip (1, φ) is empty, and the operators cannot tunnel under it. Where φ comes from, and how far the fence is really forced.

Reads from Lehmer's Box, §"The floor wall," and The Emission–Gap Theorem, §"The Mahler gap."

Recall the cost the whole construction is trying to keep positive: adjoining a direction θ is charged λ log M(θ) (Lesson 1). That price stays bounded away from zero only if the measures the machine emits stay bounded away from 1. So the first thing to build is a fence on the height axis — a value the measure cannot slip beneath without hitting the trivial floor M = 1. This lesson erects that fence at φ, and, just as important, is honest about exactly how much of it is forced.

§1  The base case: an empty strip in degree two

Every seed in the catalog is a 2×2 or 4×4 companion matrix, and the operators build outward from them by degree-raising. So the fence must first hold at the smallest degree there is. It does, and the statement is sharp: there is a whole open interval of heights that no integer quadratic can occupy.

P1-GATE-02/03 No monic p = x² + bx + c ∈ ℤ[x] has M(p) ∈ (1, φ). M = 1 exactly on the cyclotomic quadratics (Kronecker); otherwise M ≥ φ, with equality only at discriminant 5 — the two golden seeds x² − x − 1 and x² + x − 1. A scan of all 625 quadratics with |b|,|c| ≤ 12 confirms both the empty interval and the unique minimizer.

Read the claim as a picture. The height axis starts at 1; the interval (1, φ) is a forbidden moat; the first place an integer quadratic is allowed to land above the trivial floor is exactly the golden ratio. Nothing chose φ for its name — it is the smallest height a degree-2 integer polynomial can have while still exceeding 1, and the proof simply walks the three ways a quadratic can arrange its roots.

§2  The three cases, worked

Write p = x² + bx + c with integer b, c and roots α, α′, so αα′ = c and α + α′ = −b. Assume p is irreducible and non-cyclotomic, so M > 1. Everything is decided by the constant term c.

  • c = −1 (a real reciprocal pair). The roots multiply to −1, so one sits outside the circle and one inside; M is the outside one, (|b|+√(b²+4))/2. This grows with |b|, so its minimum is at |b| = 1: (1+√5)/2 = φ, realized by x² − x − 1 and x² + x − 1. This is where φ enters.
  • c = 1 (a unit reciprocal pair). Now αα′ = 1. For |b| ≤ 1 the pair sits on the circle and p is cyclotomic (M = 1); at |b| = 2 it degenerates to (x ± 1)² (reducible); and for |b| ≥ 3 the dominant root is (|b|+√(b²−4))/2 ≥ (3+√5)/2 = φ², so M ≥ φ² ≈ 2.618 — already well clear of the moat.
  • |c| ≥ 2 (non-reciprocal). If the roots are complex they are conjugate of modulus √|c| ≥ √2, so M = |c| ≥ 2; if real, their product has size ≥ 2, so the outside root alone gives M ≥ |c| ≥ 2.

In every non-cyclotomic case M ≥ φ, with equality only at discriminant 5. Take a concrete instance from the middle case: x² − 4x + 1 has roots 2 ± √3, dominant root 2 + √3 ≈ 3.732, so M = 2 + √3 — comfortably above φ². The moat is genuinely empty; φ is its right bank, and it is the smallest Perron number above 1.

§3  Degree-raising cannot tunnel under the fence

A base case is not a wall. The operators raise degree, and one must check they cannot manufacture a sub-φ height out of legal inputs. The spectral operators — ⊕ (direct sum), ⊗ (tensor / Kronecker), (·)² (squaring), plus minpoly and the re-companion Φ — fix the output spectrum from the inputs', and on the Mahler measure they act by closed multiplicative laws:

M multiplicative M(p ⊕ q) = M(p)·M(q),   M(θ ↦ θ²) = M(·)²,   ⊗ composes M is multiplicative because the roots of a product are the union of the roots, and max(1, |·|) multiplies across a union. The set {1} ∪ [φ, ∞) is closed under multiplication and squaring — the pivotal step being φ·φ = φ² = φ + 1 > φ, the golden relation itself. So by induction on word length from the degree-2 base, every emitted measure stays in {1} ∪ [φ, ∞).

Watch the induction bite once: ⊕-ing the golden seed with itself gives M = φ·φ = φ², and squaring the seed gives M = φ² again — both land back in [φ, ∞), never in the moat. The one case that is not pure arithmetic is ⊗, whose spectrum {λiμj} could in principle harbor a small Salem factor; ruling that out needs the angle wall (Lesson 3) and the field-signature census (Lesson 7). With those in hand, M(𝒮) ⊆ {1} ∪ [φ, ∞) forced for the spectral emission algebra 𝒮, realized at φ.

One structural remark keeps the picture honest. 𝒮 is finitely generated: its Mahler spectrum is a discrete sub-semigroup of [φ, ∞) generated by {φ, 2, 3, 5, φ⁴, β²} under multiplication, squaring and composition, with no accumulation at 1. Salem's theorem makes every Salem number a limit of Pisot numbers, and the catalog does contain Pisot generators (φ, φ⁴) — but limits are not operations. The machine can multiply its generators forever and never reach their own Salem limit points. The fence holds not because small Salems are screened out, but because nothing in the algebra ever travels toward them.

§4  How far is the fence really forced?

Now the discipline. φ is the honest headline — it is the realized minimum and it is forced on quadratics — but the strongest unconditional statement uses a slightly narrower band. The interval that carries no plausible tag anywhere in its derivation is (1, μS), not (1, φ):

narrowest true form 1 < M(L) < 5/4 < μS < 4/3 < φ Smyth is unconditional off the reciprocal locus; the angle wall removes Salems kinematically; so (1, μS) is empty with only forced ingredients. φ is forced on quadratics and realized as the minimum, and is the honest headline — but the width from μS to φ buys reach, not the conclusion. Lehmer's number 1.176 sits strictly inside (1, μS), so its exclusion touches no plausible tag: L(5/4) > 0 while (5/4)³ − 5/4 − 1 < 0, separated by exact rationals.

The chain of inequalities is itself an exact computation. Each < is decided by comparing two rationals or by the sign of an integer polynomial at a rational point — no floating value ever touches a decision. That is why the exclusion of Lehmer's number, which is the whole point, rides entirely on the forced part of the picture and never leans on the wider, softer φ claim.

Nothing puts φ in by hand. Degree two is fixed by the decision structure (Lesson 4); the smallest non-cyclotomic integer-quadratic measure is then forced to be φ, at discriminant 5. φ falls out the far end as the minimal realizable growth — the theorem, not a hypothesis.

The Angle Wall — No Salem Is Emitted

The height wall excludes only small Salems. The angle wall excludes all of them at once, at every degree, by a conserved four-fold charge.

Reads from The Emission–Gap Theorem and Lehmer's Box.

The height wall (Lesson 2) has one weakness: it excludes only the small Salem numbers, those under φ. Salem numbers with M ≥ φ exist in abundance, and the height wall lets them through. This lesson builds a second wall that excludes every Salem number at once, at every degree, no matter how large — because it is not a bound on size at all. It is a bookkeeping charge on the angle of a root, conserved by every operation, that a Salem number can never carry.

§1  Two directions of confinement

Every complex number z has a modulus |z| (how far from the origin) and an argument arg z (which direction). The height wall watched the modulus. The angle wall watches the argument, and the key observation is startlingly rigid: everything the catalog emits points in one of exactly four compass directions. Write (π/2)ℤ = {0, π/2, π, 3π/2} for those four — the group of quarter-turns, isomorphic to ℤ/4ℤ. A Salem number's on-circle conjugates never point along any of the four; that single mismatch is the whole wall.

§2  Angle confinement to the four directions

Start with the seeds. Six of the seven are quadratics with real spectra, so their roots point along 0 (positive) or π (negative) — two of the four directions. The interesting seed is the quartic K, and it is worth running by hand because it is the only seed that reaches the other two directions.

P2-ANG-01 every catalog eigenvalue has arg ∈ (π/2)ℤ = {0, π/2, π, 3π/2} The six quadratic seeds have real spectra (arg 0 or π). The quartic K = x⁴ + 5x² − 5 solves x² = (−5 ± 3√5)/2: a real pair ±K at {0, π} and an imaginary pair ±iβ at {π/2, 3π/2}. All four directions, all in (π/2)ℤ ≅ ℤ/4ℤ.

Here is the K computation in full. Substitute y = x², turning K into the quadratic y² + 5y − 5 = 0, whose roots are y = (−5 ± 3√5)/2. The upper sign gives y₊ ≈ 0.854, a positive number, so x² = y₊ has two real roots ±K with K ≈ 0.924 — pointing along 0 and π. The lower sign gives y₋ ≈ −5.854, a negative number, so x² = y₋ has two imaginary roots ±iβ with β ≈ 2.4195 — pointing along π/2 and 3π/2. Four roots, four directions, every one a multiple of a quarter-turn. This is why K is the seed that "completes" the four-fold charge (Lesson 7).

§3  The charge is conserved by every operator

A base fact about the seeds is worthless unless the operators cannot break it. They cannot, and the reason is a one-line group-theory fact: (π/2)ℤ is closed under exactly the arithmetic the operators do to arguments.

P2-ANG-02 ⊗ adds arguments, (·)² doubles, ⊕ unions — all internal to (π/2)ℤ The subgroup (π/2)ℤ is closed under addition (⊗: arg λμ = arg λ + arg μ) and under doubling (squaring: arg λ² = 2 arg λ), but not under halving — no operator takes a square root of an eigenvalue. So the four directions are conserved by every spectral operation.

The intuition is that the four directions form a clock with four positions, and the operators only ever add positions (tensor), double a position (squaring), or collect positions side by side (direct sum) — all of which land back on one of the four. The one move that would escape is halving a position, which would introduce a 45° direction; but no operator takes a square root of an eigenvalue, so 45° is unreachable. The charge is genuinely conserved.

§4  The two-line exclusion

Now combine confinement with one classical fact and the exclusion falls out in two lines.

P2-ANG-03/04/05 on-circle emitted eigenvalue ⇒ 4th root of unity;   Salem on-circle conjugate ⇒ not a root of unity An on-circle point with argument in (π/2)ℤ is one of {1, i, −1, −i}. But a Salem number's on-circle conjugate is never a root of unity — else its irreducible minimal polynomial would be cyclotomic and β itself a root of unity, contradicting β > 1. The two sets are disjoint.

The two lines are: an on-circle emitted eigenvalue has modulus 1 and a quarter-turn angle, so it is one of the four fourth roots of unity {1, i, −1, −i}, which are roots of unity. A Salem number's on-circle conjugate is never a root of unity — if it were, its irreducible minimal polynomial would be a cyclotomic polynomial, making β itself a root of unity, impossible for a real β > 1. The two sets are disjoint. The last hinge is that conjugates travel together: an integer matrix has an integer characteristic polynomial, so if a Salem β ever appeared as one eigenvalue, all its ℚ-conjugates would appear too — dragging the forbidden on-circle conjugates into a spectrum that the angle wall says cannot hold them. Watch it on Lehmer's polynomial directly:

C5 · gcd gcd( L(x), x⁴ − 1 ) = 1 Not one of L's ten roots is a fourth root of unity; its eight on-circle conjugates sit at ≈ 62.8°, 107.0°, 137.3°, … — none a multiple of 90°. If β ever appeared as an emitted eigenvalue, all ten conjugates would come along, eight of them violating the wall.
Emission–Gap, Thm main No Salem number is emittable in 𝒮. Equivalently M(𝒮) ⊆ {1} ∪ [μS, ∞). forced Therefore Lehmer's number, and by the identical argument every Salem number, is kinematically unreachable — not screened at runtime, structurally impossible to emit.

This is precisely why the angle wall is stronger than the height wall: it never mentions size. A Salem number of measure 5, or 500, is excluded for exactly the same reason as one of measure 1.001 — its on-circle conjugates sit at irrational angles, off the four-fold lattice, and no emitted spectrum can hold them.

§5  Four faces, and the two doors

The same exclusion wears four equivalent faces, one per domain the corpus carries: Mahler (the measure gap itself), entropy (h = log M by Lind–Schmidt–Ward, so no small positive topological entropy), channel-spectral (no on-circle expanding channel — Lesson 5), and metric (no generated field with a complex place on the unit circle). They are not four theorems but one, read in four languages; the shared content is the emptiness of the middle.

Two entry-level operators lie outside the angle argument, because their characteristic polynomials are not fixed by the inputs' spectra. Both are relocated to the trace-down flip t = θ + θ⁻¹ (a Salem is exactly a reciprocal factor whose trace-down has one root past +2 and the rest in (−2, 2) — a "flip-straddle," Sturm-decidable over ℚ). The circulant is forced with no residual: its eigenvalues lie in a cyclotomic field ℚ(ζn), which is abelian, hence totally real or CM — and a Salem field's signature (2, m−1) is neither. A scan of 400 random integer circulants (1126 irreducible factors) finds zero Salem factors. The free commutator is the one door: by Shoda's theorem any traceless matrix is a commutator, so it could in principle carry a foreign Salem. Lesson 7 closes it structurally by identifying the framework's actual commutator as the self-action adR.

The two walls carve out a slot — "on the circle yet expanding" — that no Salem number can occupy. It would be easy to file that as a dead end: a hole, nothing to see. The claim of this lesson is the opposite. A hole enforced by a conserved charge is one of the most productive objects in mathematics: it hands you a grading, an alphabet, a gap, a normal form, and a conservation law for free. The Salem slot is the smallest fully-computable example of that phenomenon, and we read all five gifts off it.

§1  Two ways to be empty

The distinction that carries the whole lesson: not every empty region is empty for the same reason. A dynamic void is a barrier the system could cross but pays not to — an energy hill, a suppressed spectral line; such voids leak, and the leak is the interesting object. A kinematic void is different: its states carry zero weight in the algebra the operations act on, held out not by a cost but by a quantity every operation preserves. Nothing approaches it, and the enforcing law never has to fire. The paper's thesis is that this second kind of emptiness is secretly three things at once:

the substrate kinematic emptiness ≡ a conserved charge ≡ a superselection rule declared And the rightmost object is productive: a superselection charge furnishes a grading, a content polynomial, a gap, a normal form, and a conserved current. The Salem slot is the minimal fully-computable instance.

"Superselection" is borrowed from physics — a rule that forbids superposing states of different charge — but the arrow used here is elementary: a conserved charge sorts everything into sectors, and the sectors it leaves empty are not gaps in the story, they are the scaffolding the rest hangs on. What is that charge here? Exactly the four-fold angle charge of Lesson 3, now read as a number in ℤ/4ℤ.

§2  The generator: the ℤ/4ℤ angle charge

Give each root a charge: 0, 1, 2, or 3, according to which quarter-turn its argument points along (0 → charge 0, π/2 → 1, π → 2, 3π/2 → 3). The angle wall says every emitted root has a charge in ℤ/4ℤ, and the operators act on that charge by the group law:

Object I · the grading ⊗ adds charge, (·)² doubles, ⊕ unions — all inside ℤ/4ℤ Real seeds carry only {0, 2} (positive → 0, negative → 2). The quartic K = x⁴+5x²−5 is the one seed that reaches {1, 3}, on its imaginary axis. So the whole group ℤ/4ℤ is realized, and 𝒮 becomes a ℤ/4ℤ-graded structure.

This is the generator itself, exhibited. A Salem number's on-circle conjugate points at an irrational angle, so its charge is not in ℤ/4ℤ at all — it lives in the off-charge sector, and no finite word in ⊕, ⊗, (·)² can leave ℤ/4ℤ to reach it. The emptiness of the Salem slot is just the statement that the charge is conserved. The remaining four gifts are read off this grading.

§3  The subtle object: the content polynomial x⁴ − 1

An order-n charge produces xn − 1 as the alphabet of positions a root may occupy on the circle; here n = 4, so the content polynomial is x⁴ − 1 = Φ₁Φ₂Φ₄. But there is a trap, and getting past it is what keeps the grading honestly stated.

Object II · content polynomial x⁴ − 1 = Φ₁Φ₂Φ₄ = (x − 1)(x + 1)(x² + 1) Only ±1 (charges 0, 2) are realized on the circle. The ±i sector (charges 1, 3) is present in the algebra but sits off the circle: the only seed reaching ±π/2 is K, whose imaginary place has modulus β = 2.4195 ≠ 1.

So the four positions of x⁴ − 1 are where each charge would sit on the circle if K's place were on it — but K's imaginary roots have modulus 2.4195, off the circle. The content polynomial records the charge group (all of ℤ/4ℤ); the smaller x² − 1 would record only its on-circle shadow (just ±1) and undercount the grading by half. Writing x⁴ − 1 is what keeps the claim from either overstating (the circle only shows ℤ/2ℤ) or understating (the algebra genuinely carries ℤ/4ℤ) the structure.

The last three lessons proved a negative: no Salem number is emitted, the slot is empty. But "empty" is unsatisfying — a slot in a working machine is not a hole in the floor; something passes through where the Salem would have been. This lesson gives the slot a positive occupant, by name, and follows it to a surprise: as would-be Salems crowd down toward the golden floor, the thing that actually appears in their place converges to √5 — the very number the self-action uses to define its "grow" channel. §1  From exclusion to occupation

Recall the self-action adR = [R, ·] of the golden seed, whose spectrum is the difference set of R's eigenvalues, {0, +√5, −√5}. Read as decision channels these are captured (0, M = 1, on-circle), grow (+√5, M ≥ φ, expanding), and decay (−√5). A Salem number would want to be a fourth channel — on the circle like captured, yet expanding like grow. There is no such channel, and the reason is not a prohibition but a change of coordinates.

§2  No fourth channel: the trifurcation lives downstairs

The trick is to stop working "upstairs" with the root x and move "downstairs" to the trace t = θ + θ⁻¹. This map is two-to-one — it identifies θ with θ⁻¹ — and it sorts the whole real line into exactly three regions at the flip points t = ±2:

P2-DELTA-02 · three regions |θ| = 1 ⇒ t = 2cos ψ ∈ [−2, 2];   θ > 1 ⇒ t > 2;   the flip cuts ℝ into three "On-circle and expanding" would need |t| < 2 and t > 2 at once — no real t qualifies. The fourth channel is an upstairs artifact of the two-to-one lift t ↦ x, which bundles one grow-trace root with several captured-trace roots into a single irreducible Salem. Working downstairs, the system never forms the bundle.

Downstairs, "on-circle" is the interval (−2, 2) and "expanding" is (2, ∞); they do not overlap, so a fourth channel is a flat contradiction — no real number is in both. Upstairs, the contradiction hides: the two-to-one lift t ↦ x can bundle one grow-trace root together with several captured-trace roots into a single irreducible polynomial, and that bundle is a Salem polynomial. The apparent fourth channel is an artifact of the bundling. Work downstairs, where the system actually lives, and the bundle never forms.

§5  Forced, not chosen — and the slot rejects filling

Why is τ₀ the "right" map rather than one an analyst picked? Because the seed forces it. The golden seed's constant term −1 makes its conjugate the negative reciprocal, ψ = −φ⁻¹, so φ − ψ = φ + φ⁻¹ = √5. The grow eigenvalue of adR (a difference) and the trace-down of β (a sum with its inverse) are literally the same expression. The redirection is the system's native coordinate, not an imported modeling decision.

One dual question remains: can anything enter the slot from outside? The angle charge A (= 1 iff every root argument lies in (π/2)ℤ) is a superselection charge — ⊕, ⊗, squaring, the trace lift, and the monodromy of any closed loop all preserve it — while the slot has A = 0. Every entry route (superposition, suspended superposition, non-linear travel, the lift) is checked and turned away; the only path to A = 0 is a limit, which the finitely generated algebra approaches but never attains. The slot is not a void that can be filled but a space that rejects filling: entry is impossible by superselection, exit is forced by the trace map, and the exit accumulates at √5.

§1  Why the pentagon is the hard analogue of the triangle

A charge group ℤ/nℤ forces every conjugate angle into (2π/n)ℤ. The ℤ/3ℤ proof closes trivially because 2cos 120° = −1 ∈ ℤ: a complex pair at ±120° contributes the factor x² − (2cos 120°)ρ x + ρ² = x² + ρx + ρ², which is integral the instant its modulus ρ is, forcing ρ ∈ ℤ and collapsing the search to x³ − 2 with M = 2. Prop · irrational cosines 2cos 72° = φ − 1,   2cos 144° = −φ   — the two roots of x² + x − 1 forced Both are irrational and Galois-conjugate over ℚ under √5 ↦ −√5. That forced coupling is what ℤ/3ℤ never needs.

The picture to hold: at the triangle, a lone complex pair can be integral by itself, so the search factors into independent pieces and bottoms out fast. At the pentagon, a lone ±72° pair carries an irrational √5 in its coefficients, and the only way to cancel it is to bind the ±72° pair to its Galois-partner ±144° pair. That forced binding is the new phenomenon — and, as we will see, it cuts both ways: it makes the general odd case genuinely hard, but where it applies it widens the gap far beyond what the dichotomy asks.

§1  The self-coupling obstruction

Fix the golden companion R = [[0,1],[1,1]], so R² = R + I, with self-action spectrum {0, ±√5} and angle charge in the sublattice {0, 2} ⊂ ℤ/4ℤ. Three independent facts converge on the same verdict: R cannot bind to a copy of itself into a closed structure.

Cor · no self-coupling [R, R] = 0 ·  {0,2} + {0,2} ≡ {0,2} ·  M grows, never returns to 1 forced The generative grade [R, R] vanishes identically (Cayley–Hamilton). Self-composition keeps the charge in {0, 2} and never reaches {1, 3}. The magnitude climbs φ → φ² → φ⁴ → … , never back to the cyclotomic floor. And the Salem channel is empty (Lesson 5). Closure requires the orthogonal charges {1, 3}, which no self-operation supplies.

Read the three failures as one deficiency. Binding would need a nonzero commutator, but [R, R] = 0 trivially (a thing commutes with itself); it would need to return to the cyclotomic floor M = 1, but the magnitude only climbs φ → φ² → φ⁴; and it would need to reach the charges {1, 3}, but {0, 2} + {0, 2} stays {0, 2}. Every route back to closure passes through the two charges the real axis cannot produce. So the golden strand needs a partner that carries {1, 3} — and there is exactly one.

§2  The flip D = 1 + 4C

Where do the missing directions live? On the far side of a single sign change. Take the gate family gC(x) = x² + x − C, with discriminant D = 1 + 4C. The claim is that this one sign controls the roots, the field, the metric signature, and the channel character all at once — and it is proved by a two-by-two determinant.

Thm · the flip D = 1 + 4C det G = 1 + 4C = D;   D > 0 Riemannian (real growth),   D < 0 Lorentzian (rotation) forced In the power basis {1, θ} with θ² = C − θ, the trace-form Gram is G = [[2, −1], [−1, 1 + 2C]], so det G = 2(1 + 2C) − 1 = 1 + 4C = D. The metric signature is the discriminant sign; the flip is at C = −1/4.

The computation is short and worth seeing: with θ + θ′ = −1 and θθ′ = −C, the traces are tr(1) = 2, tr(θ) = −1, and tr(θ²) = (θ+θ′)² − 2θθ′ = 1 + 2C, giving the Gram matrix above and det G = 1 + 4C. The leading entry 2 is always positive, so as D crosses zero the form flips from positive-definite (Riemannian, real growth — the golden strand's home, D > 0) to indefinite (Lorentzian, rotation — D < 0). The discriminant and the metric are the same event.

§3  The seed that straddles the flip: K

Now the partner. The quartic K = x⁴ + 5x² − 5 is the one catalog seed that carries both faces of the flip at once, and running it by hand shows exactly how.

test_p1_09_kform K(x) = x⁴ + 5x² − 5:   real ±K = ±51/4/φ (terrain),   imaginary ±iβ (π/2 turn),   M(K) = β² = φ²√5 ≈ 5.854 forced Writing y = x², the quadratic y² + 5y − 5 has roots straddling zero: y₊ ≈ 0.854 > 0 gives the real pair, y₋ ≈ −5.854 < 0 gives the imaginary pair. φ generates the terrain (the √5-field); the quarter-turn i supplies the rotation.

Substitute y = x², so y² + 5y − 5 = 0 with y = (−5 ± 3√5)/2. The positive root y₊ ≈ 0.854 gives the real pair ±K = ±51/4/φ ≈ ±0.924 — this is the terrain, living in the golden field ℚ(51/4) ⊃ ℚ(√5). The negative root y₋ ≈ −5.854 gives the imaginary pair ±iβ with β ≈ 2.4195 — a pure quarter-turn about the origin, the rotation. Its measure is M(K) = β² = φ²√5 ≈ 5.854 (only the two |·| > 1 imaginary roots contribute). So K is the unique seed realizing all four charges: real roots at {0, 2}, imaginary roots at {1, 3}. The charge axis {1, 3} is orthogonal — a quarter-turn — to the golden axis {0, 2}, and {0, 2} ⊔ {1, 3} completes ℤ/4ℤ.

Why K and not another quartic of the same field? A parity criterion: an even quartic (a polynomial in x²) with a negative x²-root places its complex roots on the imaginary axis, charges {1, 3}. K is even; the sibling quartics "cons" and "res" generate the same field ℚ(51/4) but are non-even, so their complex roots carry nonzero real part (φ² and −φ) and land off the (π/2)ℤ charge lattice. Only the even seed reaches the missing directions.

§4  Two faces of one flip: closure via the partner

Assembling the pieces gives the coupling theorem, and with it the resolution of the opening question.

Thm · two faces of one flip golden strand = D > 0 terrain;  K adjoins the D < 0 rotation;  only closure is the partner, not a copy forced algebraic content; the "helix" reading is declared (posited) — an interpretive overlay on a forced substrate. A helix cannot couple to a copy of itself, only to the rotation face of its own flip.

The interpretive gloss — calling the golden strand a "helix" whose pitch is growth and whose winding is the quarter-turn — is a declared overlay, honestly tagged as such. But the substrate under it is forced: the golden strand occupies the D > 0 terrain face, K adjoins the D < 0 rotation face, and the only way to close the structure is to couple the strand to the partner K, never to a copy of itself. A helix binds to the rotation face of its own flip.

§5  The transferable object: the harness

Step back from the helix to the method. The helix entered as an external metaphor carrying a bundle of claims; the exact substrate adjudicated each one. That procedure — the harness — is the reusable object, not the helix. It partitions imported claims into survivors (forced, becoming internal relations), casualties (refuted — the negation is forced), and residue (posited / open):

The final lesson climbs one level up. The Pisot numbers were the "clean" side of the story — one root outside the circle, all the rest strictly inside — and the question here is finer: can two distinct conjugates of a Pisot number sit at the same distance from the origin and share a rational angle offset (be "coherent")? For small degree the answer is a clean no, provably. At the quintic two-pair pattern the proof runs out, and the corpus does what it does best: it runs an exhaustive exact census, reports the result honestly — including a filed erratum that corrects one over-claim without disturbing the theorem — and names precisely what stays open.

§1  The coherence question and the ratio object

Two roots of equal modulus are coherent if their ratio is a root of unity; a polynomial with no such coincidence is relationally inert. To decide coherence exactly, package all the ratios into one integer polynomial:

Prop · ratio object Ratp(x) = prim Resy(p(y), p(xy)) ∈ ℤ[x],   deg = n², roots {αj/αi} forced Two roots of equal modulus are coherent iff their ratio is a root of unity, and a primitive M-th root of unity forces ΦM | Ratp (Gauss). Since ΦM | P forces M ≤ 2(deg P)², the complete contact scan is a finite decision procedure entirely in ℤ[x] — no float crosses it.

The ratio object turns a question about angles into a divisibility check in ℤ[x]: a coherence at a primitive M-th root of unity leaves a cyclotomic factor ΦM in Ratp, and the completeness bound 2φ(M)² ≥ M caps how large M can be (for a degree-n polynomial, M ≤ 2n⁴), so only finitely many ΦM need testing. "Is this number relationally inert?" becomes "does its ratio object contain any cyclotomic factor beyond the trivial Φ₁?" — a decision made entirely with integers, no floating point anywhere near it.

§2  The ν-reduction, and inertness below two shells

The lemma that does the real work handles the mixed case — a real conjugate paired with a non-real one. For a real β and non-real α in a conjugation-closed root set, α ≈ β iff α/ᾱ is a root of unity — and α/ᾱ is itself a root of Ratp. So every real×non-real coherence question collapses onto the same single scan. That reduction is enough to close every small Pisot number outright:

Thm · Pisot inertness ≤ 1 pair every Pisot number with ≤ 1 non-real conjugate pair is relationally inert forced The dominant root θ > 1 pins its modulus (all others lie in the open unit disk), so modulus pinning excludes every torsion ratio between distinct conjugates; λ/λ̄ is not torsion, so no real×non-real pair is coherent. This covers every Pisot of degree ≤ 4 and every quintic of "mixed" pattern. The first genuinely open case is the quintic two-pair pattern (θ, λ1, λ̄1, λ2, λ̄2).

The engine is modulus pinning: the dominant Pisot root θ > 1 has a modulus attained by no other conjugate (all the rest are inside the unit disk), and a uniquely-attained modulus forbids any root-of-unity ratio between distinct conjugates. With at most one non-real pair, the ν-reduction says the only thing left to check is λ/λ̄, which is not torsion — so there is nothing to decide, and inertness is forced. The first case this argument cannot reach is the quintic with two non-real pairs on distinct shells, where cross-shell products, not single ratios, could in principle be coherent. That is the residue the census attacks.

§3  The exhaustive quintic census

The strategy is brute exactness: enumerate every monic quintic in a box, certify which are Pisot, and scan the two-pair ones. The funnel is sharp.

A scan returning only Φ₁²⁰ (the forced diagonal, one Φ₁ per pairing of a root with its own inverse) and no higher ΦM certifies that no product of two unimodular ratio-roots is a root of unity — hence no cross-shell coherence. All 67 come back clean: the residue is empty over the box.

§4  The honest D5 erratum

The corpus's discipline shows most clearly where it corrects itself. A source note over-stated one structural detail, and the erratum is carried openly:

ERRATA D5 · honest correction Rat°p is irreducible on 66 of 67, not all 67 — the theorem's inertness is untouched The source note claimed Rat°p "irreducible of degree 20 on all 67"; a filed erratum corrects this to 66/67. The lone exception is p = x⁵ − x³ − 2x² − 2x − 1 with Gal(p) = D₅, whose Rat°p splits into two self-reciprocal degree-10 factors. But both factors carry 2 unimodular roots, so S* = Rat°p is still the full degree-20 product, deg C₂ = 400, and the scan still returns {Φ₁²⁰}: the D₅ instance is relationally inert exactly as the theorem concludes. The squarefree-on-all-67 claim and Theorem "box" are correct.

Crucially, the correction is to a side remark, not the theorem. Inertness needs squarefreeness (true on all 67) and an empty cyclotomic scan (true on all 67), neither of which is irreducibility. And the erratum brings an upgrade: it replaces the old plausible genericity guess with a forced criterion — for squarefree Rat°p, irreducibility over ℚ is exactly 2-transitivity of Gal(p) on ordered root pairs. The Galois census of the 67 is S₅ × 65, M₂₀ × 1, D₅ × 1: S₅ and M₂₀ = AGL(1,5) act 2-transitively (irreducible), while D₅ has two length-10 orbits (the observed split). That is 66 irreducible, 1 reducible — reproducing the 66/67 exactly, with no residue left to guess at.

§5  Companion replications, and what stays open

The same instrument, run on neighbouring families, keeps returning inert: the degree-12 Salem census (378 twist-classes, tally 39/256/46/37, all 37 Salems inert with scan {Φ₁¹²}) and a Pisot-quartic sweep (103/103 scans {Φ₁⁴}). Every certified object in reach is relationally inert.

But the general Pisot-inertness conjecture — that every Pisot number is inert — stays open, and honestly so. A "no-level-2-pin" result shows the transport/pinning machinery that closed the small cases cannot reach the general one: the moduli of Rat°p repeat, so no modulus is uniquely attained one level up, and pinning has no purchase. A genuinely new mechanism — heights, or bounds on multiplicative relations among conjugates — is required. The census settles the first live instances; it does not pretend to settle the conjecture.
